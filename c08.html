Chapter 8

Derived functors in practice: Ext and Tor

In this chapter

  1. 8.1 Resolutions, and what is special about Z\ZZZ
  2. 8.2 Tor⁡\TorTor, flatness, and balance
  3. 8.3 Spaces as algebraic devices
  4. 8.4 Künneth, and non-naturality again

Part I used Ext⁡\ExtExt and Tor⁡\TorTor as bookkeeping: a term in the universal coefficient sequence, a term in Künneth. This chapter treats them as what they are — derived functors — and the change of viewpoint pays off twice. It explains which of their properties are theorems and which are accidents of the ring Z\ZZZ; and it makes them computable in situations where no exact sequence is handed to us.

The second half of the chapter runs the traffic in the unusual direction. A space with prescribed homology is an algebraic device: build one, compute its homology in two ways, and out comes a theorem about abelian groups. That manoeuvre is the point of Section 8.3, and it is worth more than either of the particular results proved with it.

8.1 Resolutions, and what is special about Z\ZZZ

Definition 8.1. A free resolution of an RRR-module AAA is an exact sequence

⋯→F2→F1→F0→A→0\cdots\to F_2\to F_1\to F_0\to A\to0⋯→F2​→F1​→F0​→A→0

with each FiF_iFi​ free. Applying Hom⁡R(−,B)\Hom_R(-,B)HomR​(−,B) to the deleted resolution and taking cohomology gives Ext⁡Rn(A,B)\Ext^n_R(A,B)ExtRn​(A,B); applying −⊗RB-\otimes_RB−⊗R​B and taking homology gives Tor⁡nR(A,B)\Tor^R_n(A,B)TornR​(A,B).

Proposition 8.2 (Comparison; recalled). Any two free resolutions of AAA are chain homotopy equivalent, by an equivalence lifting the identity of AAA and unique up to chain homotopy. Consequently Ext⁡Rn(A,B)\Ext^n_R(A,B)ExtRn​(A,B) and Tor⁡nR(A,B)\Tor^R_n(A,B)TornR​(A,B) do not depend on the resolution chosen.

Over Z\ZZZ something special happens, and it is worth naming so that it can later be taken away.

Definition 8.3. A ring is hereditary if every submodule of a free module is projective.

Example 8.4 (Z\ZZZ is hereditary). Every subgroup of a free abelian group is free abelian. Hence for every abelian group AAA the kernel F1F_1F1​ of a surjection F0↠AF_0\twoheadrightarrow AF0​↠A from a free group is itself free, and

0⟶F1⟶F0⟶A⟶00\longrightarrow F_1\longrightarrow F_0\longrightarrow A\longrightarrow00⟶F1​⟶F0​⟶A⟶0

is a free resolution of length one. Therefore

Ext⁡Zn(A,B)=0andTor⁡nZ(A,B)=0for all n≥2,\Ext^n_{\ZZ}(A,B)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\Tor^{\ZZ}_n(A,B)=0 \qquad\text{for all }n\ge2 ,ExtZn​(A,B)=0andTornZ​(A,B)=0for all n≥2,

which is why Part I could write Ext⁡\ExtExt and Tor⁡\TorTor with no index at all.

Example 8.5 (The standard computations). From 0→Z→mZ→Z/m→00\to\ZZ\xrightarrow{m}\ZZ\to\ZZ/m\to00→Zm​Z→Z/m→0 one reads off

Ext⁡(Z/m,Z)≅Z/m,Ext⁡(Z/m,Z/n)≅Z/d,Tor⁡(Z/m,Z/n)≅Z/d,\Ext(\ZZ/m,\ZZ)\cong\ZZ/m,\qquad \Ext(\ZZ/m,\ZZ/n)\cong\ZZ/d,\qquad \Tor(\ZZ/m,\ZZ/n)\cong\ZZ/d,Ext(Z/m,Z)≅Z/m,Ext(Z/m,Z/n)≅Z/d,Tor(Z/m,Z/n)≅Z/d,

where d=gcd⁡(m,n)d=\gcd(m,n)d=gcd(m,n); and both vanish as soon as the first variable is free.

Now remove the hypothesis.

Exercise 8.6 (medium). Let kkk be a field and R=k[x]/(x2)R=k[x]/(x^2)R=k[x]/(x2), the ring of dual numbers, and regard k=R/(x)k=R/(x)k=R/(x) as an RRR-module. Show that

Ext⁡Rn(k,k)≅kfor every n≥0.\Ext^n_R(k,k)\cong k\qquad\text{for every }n\ge0 .ExtRn​(k,k)≅kfor every n≥0.

Hint. Multiplication by xxx has kernel exactly (x)(x)(x), which is also its image. What does that say about the sequence ⋯→R→xR→xR\cdots\to R\xrightarrow{x}R\xrightarrow{x}R⋯→Rx​Rx​R?

Solution. In RRR we have ker⁡(⋅x)=(x)=im⁡(⋅x)\ker(\cdot x)=(x)=\im(\cdot x)ker(⋅x)=(x)=im(⋅x), so

⋯⟶R→ ⋅x R→ ⋅x R⟶k⟶0\cdots\longrightarrow R\xrightarrow{\ \cdot x\ }R\xrightarrow{\ \cdot x\ }R \longrightarrow k\longrightarrow0⋯⟶R ⋅x ​R ⋅x ​R⟶k⟶0

is exact: the augmentation R→k=R/(x)R\to k=R/(x)R→k=R/(x) has kernel (x)(x)(x), which is the image of the first ⋅x\cdot x⋅x, and exactness at every later spot is the displayed equality. So kkk admits a free resolution which repeats itself with period one.

Apply Hom⁡R(−,k)\Hom_R(-,k)HomR​(−,k). Each Hom⁡R(R,k)=k\Hom_R(R,k)=kHomR​(R,k)=k, and the induced map is multiplication by xxx on kkk, which is zero because xxx acts as 000 on R/(x)R/(x)R/(x). So the complex computing Ext⁡\ExtExt is k→0k→0k→⋯k\xrightarrow{0}k\xrightarrow{0}k\to\cdotsk0​k0​k→⋯, and Ext⁡Rn(k,k)=k\Ext^n_R(k,k)=kExtRn​(k,k)=k for every n≥0n\ge0n≥0.

Remark 8.7. A periodic resolution produces periodic Ext⁡\ExtExt, and periodicity is common: it happens for k[x]/(x2)k[x]/(x^2)k[x]/(x2) above, for cyclic groups over Z\ZZZ in group cohomology (Chapter 13 uses H∗(Z/p)H^*(\ZZ/p)H∗(Z/p) being nonzero in every degree), and for any ring where some element has annihilator equal to its own ideal. The general lesson is that “how long is a resolution” is a question about the ring, and Example 8.4 is the accident that lets one forget it over Z\ZZZ.

8.2 Tor⁡\TorTor, flatness, and balance

Tor⁡\TorTor may be computed by resolving either variable, and the resolution need not be free.

Theorem 8.8 (Balance and flat resolutions; recalled). Tor⁡nR(A,B)\Tor^R_n(A,B)TornR​(A,B) may be computed from a projective resolution of AAA or of BBB, with canonically isomorphic results. More generally it may be computed from a resolution of either variable by flat modules.

Example 8.9. Compute Tor⁡(Z/m,Z/n)\Tor(\ZZ/m,\ZZ/n)Tor(Z/m,Z/n) twice. Resolving the first variable by 0→Z→mZ→Z/m→00\to\ZZ\xrightarrow{m}\ZZ\to\ZZ/m\to00→Zm​Z→Z/m→0 and tensoring with Z/n\ZZ/nZ/n gives Z/n→mZ/n\ZZ/n\xrightarrow{m}\ZZ/nZ/nm​Z/n, whose kernel is Z/ ⁣gcd⁡(m,n)\ZZ/\!\gcd(m,n)Z/gcd(m,n). Resolving the second variable gives Z/m→nZ/m\ZZ/m\xrightarrow{n}\ZZ/mZ/mn​Z/m, whose kernel is again Z/ ⁣gcd⁡(m,n)\ZZ/\!\gcd(m,n)Z/gcd(m,n). The two computations look different and agree, which is what Theorem 8.8 promises.

The flat-resolution clause is not a technicality; it is what makes the non-finitely-generated cases computable at all, because the useful resolutions there are rarely free.

Exercise 8.10 (medium). Show that a torsion-free abelian group AAA is flat, and deduce Tor⁡(A,B)=0\Tor(A,B)=0Tor(A,B)=0 for all BBB. In particular Q\QQQ is flat, so

0⟶Z⟶Q⟶Q/Z⟶00\longrightarrow\ZZ\longrightarrow\QQ\longrightarrow\QQ/\ZZ\longrightarrow00⟶Z⟶Q⟶Q/Z⟶0

is a flat resolution of Q/Z\QQ/\ZZQ/Z of length one.

Hint. A finitely generated torsion-free abelian group is free. And AAA is the filtered union of its finitely generated subgroups.

Solution. Write A=lim→⁡AiA=\varinjlim A_iA=lim​Ai​ over its finitely generated subgroups, a filtered colimit. Each AiA_iAi​ is finitely generated and torsion-free, hence free, hence flat. Tensor products commute with filtered colimits and filtered colimits are exact, so A⊗−A\otimes-A⊗− is exact: AAA is flat. Equivalently Tor⁡1(A,B)=lim→⁡Tor⁡1(Ai,B)=0\Tor_1(A,B)=\varinjlim\Tor_1(A_i,B)=0Tor1​(A,B)=lim​Tor1​(Ai​,B)=0.

Q\QQQ is torsion-free, so it is flat, and the displayed sequence is then a resolution of Q/Z\QQ/\ZZQ/Z by flat modules, of length one. By Theorem 8.8 it computes Tor⁡(−,Q/Z)\Tor(-,\QQ/\ZZ)Tor(−,Q/Z).

Exercise 8.11 (easy). Show that the kernel of A→A⊗QA\to A\otimes\QQA→A⊗Q, a↦a⊗1a\mapsto a\otimes1a↦a⊗1, is exactly the torsion subgroup of AAA.

Solution. If na=0na=0na=0 with n≠0n\neq0n=0 then a⊗1=a⊗nn=na⊗1n=0a\otimes1=a\otimes\frac{n}{n}=na\otimes\frac1n=0a⊗1=a⊗nn​=na⊗n1​=0, so torsion elements die. Conversely A⊗QA\otimes\QQA⊗Q is the localisation of AAA at Z∖{0}\ZZ\setminus\{0\}Z∖{0}, in which a⊗1=0a\otimes1=0a⊗1=0 precisely when na=0na=0na=0 for some n≠0n\neq0n=0; so the kernel consists of torsion elements only.

Remark 8.12. Exercises 8.10 and 8.11 fit together. Applying A⊗−A\otimes-A⊗− to the flat resolution 0→Z→Q→Q/Z→00\to\ZZ\to\QQ\to\QQ/\ZZ\to00→Z→Q→Q/Z→0 makes Tor⁡(A,Q/Z)\Tor(A,\QQ/\ZZ)Tor(A,Q/Z) the kernel of A⊗Z→A⊗QA\otimes\ZZ\to A\otimes\QQA⊗Z→A⊗Q, and Exercise 8.11 identifies that kernel. Carrying this out, and then deducing a characterisation of torsion-freeness in terms of the vanishing of Tor⁡\TorTor, is the natural exercise to set oneself here; both ingredients are on this page.

8.3 Spaces as algebraic devices

Moore spaces (Proposition 6.4) have homology concentrated in one degree, for an arbitrary prescribed group. That makes them instruments: compute an invariant of a Moore space in two different ways, and the comparison is a theorem of algebra.

Example 8.13 (Tor⁡\TorTor realised as a homology group). Let n,m≥1n,m\ge1n,m≥1 and put X=M(A,n)X=M(A,n)X=M(A,n), Y=M(B,m)Y=M(B,m)Y=M(B,m). Künneth gives

Hk(X×Y)≅⨁i+j=kHi(X)⊗Hj(Y) ⊕⨁i+j=k−1Tor⁡(Hi(X),Hj(Y)).H_k(X\times Y)\cong\bigoplus_{i+j=k}H_i(X)\otimes H_j(Y) \ \oplus\bigoplus_{i+j=k-1}\Tor\big(H_i(X),H_j(Y)\big).Hk​(X×Y)≅i+j=k⨁​Hi​(X)⊗Hj​(Y) ⊕i+j=k−1⨁​Tor(Hi​(X),Hj​(Y)).

The only nonzero reduced homology of XXX is AAA in degree nnn, and of YYY is BBB in degree mmm. In degree k=n+m+1k=n+m+1k=n+m+1 the tensor summands all vanish — the available degrees are 000, nnn, mmm and n+mn+mn+m, and n+m+1n+m+1n+m+1 is none of them — while the Tor⁡\TorTor summands require i+j=n+mi+j=n+mi+j=n+m, leaving exactly Tor⁡(A,B)\Tor(A,B)Tor(A,B). Hence

Hn+m+1(M(A,n)×M(B,m))  ≅  Tor⁡(A,B).H_{n+m+1}\big(M(A,n)\times M(B,m)\big)\;\cong\;\Tor(A,B).Hn+m+1​(M(A,n)×M(B,m))≅Tor(A,B).

Exercise 8.14 (medium). Deduce that Tor⁡(A,B)≅Tor⁡(B,A)\Tor(A,B)\cong\Tor(B,A)Tor(A,B)≅Tor(B,A) for all abelian groups AAA and BBB, without using a resolution.

Hint. X×YX\times YX×Y and Y×XY\times XY×X are homeomorphic.

Solution. Take n=mn=mn=m in Example 8.13, so that Tor⁡(A,B)≅H2n+1(M(A,n)×M(B,n))\Tor(A,B)\cong H_{2n+1}(M(A,n)\times M(B,n))Tor(A,B)≅H2n+1​(M(A,n)×M(B,n)) and Tor⁡(B,A)≅H2n+1(M(B,n)×M(A,n))\Tor(B,A)\cong H_{2n+1}(M(B,n)\times M(A,n))Tor(B,A)≅H2n+1​(M(B,n)×M(A,n)). The swap map (u,v)↦(v,u)(u,v)\mapsto(v,u)(u,v)↦(v,u) is a homeomorphism between the two spaces, so it induces an isomorphism on homology in every degree, and in particular in degree 2n+12n+12n+1.

Remark 8.15 (The method, stated plainly). The manoeuvre has three steps: realise an algebraic functor as a homology group of a space built to order; find a second computation of that homology group; and read the comparison as an algebraic identity. Example 8.13 realises Tor⁡\TorTor; the second computation in Exercise 8.14 was a homeomorphism. Other second computations are available — coefficients in a field, where the universal coefficient theorem loses its Ext⁡\ExtExt term, is the most useful one, and it converts a vanishing statement about the cohomology of a Moore space directly into the vanishing of an Ext⁡\ExtExt group. Proposition 6.4 builds the space for any group at all, so nothing here requires finite generation.

8.4 Künneth, and non-naturality again

Theorem 8.16 (Künneth; recalled). For CW complexes X,YX,YX,Y there is a short exact sequence, natural in both variables,

0→⨁i+j=kHi(X)⊗Hj(Y)→Hk(X×Y)→⨁i+j=k−1Tor⁡(Hi(X),Hj(Y))→0,0\to\bigoplus_{i+j=k}H_i(X)\otimes H_j(Y)\to H_k(X\times Y) \to\bigoplus_{i+j=k-1}\Tor\big(H_i(X),H_j(Y)\big)\to0 ,0→i+j=k⨁​Hi​(X)⊗Hj​(Y)→Hk​(X×Y)→i+j=k−1⨁​Tor(Hi​(X),Hj​(Y))→0,

and it splits — but not naturally.

Example 8.17 (A Künneth drill). H∗(RP2)=(Z, Z/2, 0)H_*(\RP^2)=(\ZZ,\ \ZZ/2,\ 0)H∗​(RP2)=(Z, Z/2, 0), so for X=Y=RP2X=Y=\RP^2X=Y=RP2:

H∗(RP2×RP2)=(Z, (Z/2)2, Z/2, Z/2, 0).H_*(\RP^2\times\RP^2)=\big(\ZZ,\ (\ZZ/2)^2,\ \ZZ/2,\ \ZZ/2,\ 0\big).H∗​(RP2×RP2)=(Z, (Z/2)2, Z/2, Z/2, 0).

Degrees 111 and 222 come from tensor summands, and the Z/2\ZZ/2Z/2 in degree 333 comes from Tor⁡(Z/2,Z/2)\Tor(\ZZ/2,\ZZ/2)Tor(Z/2,Z/2) alone: there is no tensor contribution in that degree at all. A class living in a degree where the tensor part vanishes is exactly what a natural splitting would have to keep track of.

Proposition 8.18. Suppose the splitting of Theorem 8.16 could be chosen naturally in both variables. Then for maps f ⁣:X→X′f\colon X\to X'f:X→X′ and g ⁣:Y→Y′g\colon Y\to Y'g:Y→Y′ the induced map (f×g)∗(f\times g)_*(f×g)∗​ would decompose as (⨁f∗⊗g∗)⊕(⨁Tor⁡(f∗,g∗))\big(\bigoplus f_*\otimes g_*\big)\oplus\big(\bigoplus\Tor(f_*,g_*)\big)(⨁f∗​⊗g∗​)⊕(⨁Tor(f∗​,g∗​)). In particular, were f∗f_*f∗​ to vanish on H~∗\widetilde H_*H∗​, every summand built from positive-degree homology of XXX would be killed by (f×g)∗(f\times g)_*(f×g)∗​.

Proof. A natural splitting identifies the middle term with the sum of the two outer ones, compatibly with induced maps. On each outer term the induced map is the functor ⊗\otimes⊗ or Tor⁡\TorTor evaluated at the pair (f∗,g∗)(f_*,g_*)(f∗​,g∗​), and both vanish as soon as f∗f_*f∗​ does.□

Remark 8.19 (What to do with it). Proposition 8.18 is the exact analogue of Proposition 6.7, and it is refuted in exactly the same way: exhibit one map that is zero on reduced homology and one product in which it is not zero. Chapter 6 supplied both the collapse map M(Z/m,n)→Sn+1M(\ZZ/m,n)\to S^{n+1}M(Z/m,n)→Sn+1 and the computation showing what it does to cohomology; Example 8.17 shows where in a product a Tor⁡\TorTor class can sit with no tensor class beside it. Crossing such a collapse with the identity and asking what happens to the Tor⁡\TorTor summand is the natural experiment, and the reader who ran Remark 6.12 has already done its harder half.

The pattern is now established twice, and it is worth stating as a slogan: an Ext⁡\ExtExt or Tor⁡\TorTor summand records information about a space that its homology groups do not determine as a functor. Everything in Chapter 18 is an attempt to name that information.

8.5 What to carry forward

  • Resolutions compute derived functors and the answer is resolution-independent (Proposition 8.2); over Z\ZZZ they have length one, and that is a property of Z\ZZZ (Example 8.4 and Exercise 8.6).

  • Tor⁡\TorTor is balanced and may be computed by flat resolutions (Theorem 8.8); torsion-free abelian groups are flat (Exercise 8.10).

  • A Moore space realises Tor⁡\TorTor as a homology group (Example 8.13); comparing two computations of that group proves algebra (Remark 8.15).

  • Künneth is natural and splits, unnaturally (Theorem 8.16 and Proposition 8.18) — the second instance of the pattern opened in Chapter 6.
