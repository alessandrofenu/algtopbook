Chapter 13

Whitehead theorems, Postnikov towers, and what the invariants miss

Chapter 5 ended by observing that homology cannot see the universal cover. This chapter says what does. Whitehead’s theorem is the statement that homotopy groups, together with a map realising them, form a complete invariant of CW homotopy type; the rest of the chapter is about how much of that completeness survives when one of those two ingredients is weakened.

Every result here has a hypothesis that looks removable and is not. The reader should finish the chapter able to name, for each theorem, the space or map that goes wrong when the hypothesis is dropped.

13.1 Whitehead’s theorem, and the map it needs

Theorem 13.1 (Whitehead; recalled). A map f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y between connected CW complexes that induces isomorphisms πn(X,x0)→πn(Y,f(x0))\pi_n(X,x_0)\to\pi_n(Y,f(x_0))πn​(X,x0​)→πn​(Y,f(x0​)) for all n≥1n\ge1n≥1 is a homotopy equivalence.

Warning 13.2 (The map is not optional). The theorem is about a map. Two spaces can have isomorphic homotopy groups in every degree and fail to be homotopy equivalent, and the book has already met such a pair.

Take the lens spaces L(5,1)L(5,1)L(5,1) and L(5,2)L(5,2)L(5,2) of Example 2.21. Both have fundamental group Z/5\ZZ/5Z/5 and universal cover S3S^3S3, so for n≥2n\ge2n≥2

πn(L(5,q))≅πn(S3)\pi_n\big(L(5,q)\big)\cong\pi_n(S^3)πn​(L(5,q))≅πn​(S3)

by the covering-space isomorphism, independently of qqq; and π1\pi_1π1​ agrees too. So all their homotopy groups are isomorphic. Their homology agrees (Example 4.8) and so does their cohomology ring (Example 7.10). They are nevertheless not homotopy equivalent.

What is missing is not an invariant but a map: no map between them induces those isomorphisms.

Exercise 13.3 (easy). Let XXX be a connected CW complex with πn(X)=0\pi_n(X)=0πn​(X)=0 for all n≥1n\ge1n≥1. Show XXX is contractible.

Solution. Apply Theorem 13.1 to the inclusion of a point {x0}↪X\{x_0\}\hookrightarrow X{x0​}↪X, which induces isomorphisms 0→πn(X)0\to\pi_n(X)0→πn​(X) in every degree by hypothesis. Both spaces are CW complexes, so the inclusion is a homotopy equivalence.

13.2 Homology, and the universal cover

Homotopy groups are hard to compute and homology is easy, so one wants a homological criterion. There is one, with a hypothesis.

Theorem 13.4 (Homological Whitehead; recalled). Let f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y be a map of simply connected CW complexes inducing an isomorphism on Hn(−;Z)H_n(-;\ZZ)Hn​(−;Z) for every nnn. Then fff is a homotopy equivalence.

Proof sketch. Replace fff by an inclusion using the mapping cylinder. The homology of the pair vanishes, and the pair is simply connected, so the relative Hurewicz theorem gives πn(Y,X)=0\pi_n(Y,X)=0πn​(Y,X)=0 for all nnn; the long exact sequence of the pair then makes f∗f_*f∗​ an isomorphism on homotopy groups, and Theorem 13.1 applies.□

Warning 13.5 (Simple connectivity is essential). Let PPP be the Poincaré homology sphere: a closed orientable 333-manifold with π1(P)\pi_1(P)π1​(P) the binary icosahedral group of order 120120120, and with H∗(P;Z)≅H∗(S3;Z)H_*(P;\ZZ)\cong H_*(S^3;\ZZ)H∗​(P;Z)≅H∗​(S3;Z). Collapsing the complement of an open ball gives a degree-one map

c ⁣:P⟶P/(P∖B3)≅S3,c\colon P\longrightarrow P/(P\setminus B^3)\cong S^3 ,c:P⟶P/(P∖B3)≅S3,

which is an isomorphism on H3H_3H3​ because it has degree one, and trivially so in the other degrees, both spaces having the homology of a sphere. So c∗c_*c∗​ is an isomorphism on all integral homology.

But ccc is not a homotopy equivalence: π1(P)\pi_1(P)π1​(P) has order 120120120 and π1(S3)\pi_1(S^3)π1​(S3) is trivial. The single hypothesis that fails is simple connectivity, and it fails on the source.

The repair promised in Remark 5.18 is to work upstairs.

Proposition 13.6. Let f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y be a map of connected CW complexes inducing an isomorphism on π1\pi_1π1​, and let f~ ⁣:X~→Y~\tilde f\colon\widetilde X\to\widetilde Yf~​:X→Y be a lift to the universal covers. If f~∗\tilde f_*f~​∗​ is an isomorphism on homotopy groups, then fff is a homotopy equivalence.

Proof. A covering induces isomorphisms on πn\pi_nπn​ for n≥2n\ge2n≥2, and the square formed by fff, f~\tilde ff~​ and the two covering projections commutes. So f∗f_*f∗​ is an isomorphism on πn\pi_nπn​ for n≥2n\ge2n≥2, and by hypothesis also on π1\pi_1π1​. Apply Theorem 13.1.□

Exercise 13.7 (easy). Prove the fact used twice above: a covering p ⁣:X~→Xp\colon\widetilde X\to Xp:X→X induces isomorphisms πn(X~)→πn(X)\pi_n(\widetilde X)\to\pi_n(X)πn​(X)→πn​(X) for all n≥2n\ge2n≥2.

Hint. Lift maps Sn→XS^n\to XSn→X and homotopies Sn×I→XS^n\times I\to XSn×I→X, using that SnS^nSn is simply connected for n≥2n\ge2n≥2.

Solution. For surjectivity, a map Sn→XS^n\to XSn→X with n≥2n\ge2n≥2 lifts to X~\widetilde XX because SnS^nSn is simply connected, so the lifting criterion applies. For injectivity, if two lifts become homotopic downstairs, the homotopy Sn×I→XS^n\times I\to XSn×I→X lifts (again by simple connectivity of Sn×IS^n\times ISn×I) to a homotopy upstairs starting at the given lift, and uniqueness of lifts identifies its other end with the second lift.

Remark 13.8 (The invariant that works). Proposition 13.6 is stated in terms of homotopy groups upstairs, but the useful version replaces them by homology. The homology of X~\widetilde XX is not merely a graded abelian group: the deck group π1(X)\pi_1(X)π1​(X) acts on it, making it a module over the group ring Z[π1X]\ZZ[\pi_1X]Z[π1​X] — this is what Example 5.16 exhibited for S1∨S2S^1\vee S^2S1∨S2, where H2(X~)H_2(\widetilde X)H2​(X) was free of rank one over Z[t,t−1]\ZZ[t,t^{-1}]Z[t,t−1]. That module is homology with local coefficients, H∗(X;Z[π1X])H_*(X;\ZZ[\pi_1X])H∗​(X;Z[π1​X]).

Combining Proposition 13.6 with Theorem 13.4 applied upstairs — the universal cover being simply connected — turns a homological hypothesis on X~\widetilde XX into a homotopy equivalence downstairs. Assembling that statement, and checking exactly which hypothesis on π1\pi_1π1​ it needs, is the natural exercise at this point, and it is the invariant Chapter 5 promised.

13.3 Postnikov towers

Whitehead’s theorem says the homotopy groups determine the homotopy type once a map is given. Postnikov towers organise the homotopy groups so that the glue between them becomes visible as well.

Theorem 13.9 (Postnikov towers; recalled). For a connected CW complex XXX there are spaces XnX^nXn and maps forming a commutative tower under XXX,

X⟶⋯⟶Xn⟶Xn−1⟶⋯⟶X1,X\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow X^{n}\longrightarrow X^{n-1} \longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow X^{1},X⟶⋯⟶Xn⟶Xn−1⟶⋯⟶X1,

such that X→XnX\to X^nX→Xn induces isomorphisms on πi\pi_iπi​ for i≤ni\le ni≤n, and πi(Xn)=0\pi_i(X^n)=0πi​(Xn)=0 for i>ni>ni>n. Each map Xn→Xn−1X^n\to X^{n-1}Xn→Xn−1 is a fibration with fibre K(πnX,n)K(\pi_nX,n)K(πn​X,n), and it is pulled back from the path fibration over K(πnX,n+1)K(\pi_nX,n+1)K(πn​X,n+1) along a map

kn ⁣:Xn−1⟶K(πnX, n+1),k^{n}\colon X^{n-1}\longrightarrow K(\pi_nX,\,n+1),kn:Xn−1⟶K(πn​X,n+1),

the kkk-invariant, a class in Hn+1(Xn−1;πnX)H^{n+1}\big(X^{n-1};\pi_nX\big)Hn+1(Xn−1;πn​X). The stages and the tower are unique up to homotopy over XXX.

Dually, the nnn-connected cover X⟨n⟩→XX\langle n\rangle\to XX⟨n⟩→X kills the homotopy below degree n+1n+1n+1 and leaves the rest alone.

Exercise 13.10 (easy). Show that the nnn-connected cover X⟨n⟩→XX\langle n\rangle\to XX⟨n⟩→X induces isomorphisms on πi\pi_iπi​ for i>ni>ni>n and satisfies πi(X⟨n⟩)=0\pi_i(X\langle n\rangle)=0πi​(X⟨n⟩)=0 for i≤ni\le ni≤n. Then identify X⟨2⟩X\langle2\rangleX⟨2⟩ when XXX is simply connected.

Solution. The defining property of the connected cover is exactly the pair of statements about πi\pi_iπi​; they are what the construction (attach cells of dimension ≥n+2\ge n+2≥n+2 to kill nothing, or take the fibre of X→XnX\to X^nX→Xn) achieves. Taking the fibre of X→XnX\to X^nX→Xn realises X⟨n⟩X\langle n\rangleX⟨n⟩, and the long exact sequence of that fibration gives both claims.

For XXX simply connected and n=2n=2n=2: X2=K(π2X,2)X^2=K(\pi_2X,2)X2=K(π2​X,2), so the fibre sequence X⟨2⟩→X→K(π2X,2)X\langle2\rangle\to X\to K(\pi_2X,2)X⟨2⟩→X→K(π2​X,2) exhibits X⟨2⟩X\langle2\rangleX⟨2⟩ as the 222-connected cover, with πi\pi_iπi​ equal to πi(X)\pi_i(X)πi​(X) for i≥3i\ge3i≥3 and zero below.

Example 13.11 (A two-stage tower). Let XXX be simply connected with π2X=π3X=Z\pi_2X=\pi_3X=\ZZπ2​X=π3​X=Z and all other homotopy groups zero. Then X2=K(Z,2)=CP∞X^2=K(\ZZ,2)=\CP^\inftyX2=K(Z,2)=CP∞, and X=X3X=X^3X=X3 sits in a fibration

K(Z,3)⟶X⟶CP∞K(\ZZ,3)\longrightarrow X\longrightarrow\CP^\inftyK(Z,3)⟶X⟶CP∞

classified by a single kkk-invariant

k∈H4(CP∞;Z)=Z⋅ι2,k\in H^4\big(\CP^\infty;\ZZ\big)=\ZZ\cdot\iota^2 ,k∈H4(CP∞;Z)=Z⋅ι2,

where ι∈H2(CP∞;Z)\iota\in H^2(\CP^\infty;\ZZ)ι∈H2(CP∞;Z) is the fundamental class and ι2\iota^2ι2 its square. So such spaces are classified by an integer. The value k=0k=0k=0 gives the product CP∞×K(Z,3)\CP^\infty\times K(\ZZ,3)CP∞×K(Z,3); the value k=ι2k=\iota^2k=ι2 gives the 333-type of S2S^2S2, and the appearance of a cup square as a kkk-invariant is the homotopy-theoretic shadow of the Hopf map.

Exercise 13.12 (medium). Show that a two-stage Postnikov tower with vanishing kkk-invariant is a product of Eilenberg–MacLane spaces.

Hint. The stage is the pullback of the path fibration along kkk. What is the pullback of a fibration along a constant map?

Solution. By Theorem 13.9 the stage Xn→Xn−1X^n\to X^{n-1}Xn→Xn−1 is the pullback of the path fibration PK(π,n+1)→K(π,n+1)PK(\pi,n+1)\to K(\pi,n+1)PK(π,n+1)→K(π,n+1) along knk^nkn. If knk^nkn is nullhomotopic the pullback is, up to homotopy, the pullback along a constant map, which is the product of the base with the fibre. With Xn−1=K(πn−1,n−1)X^{n-1}=K(\pi_{n-1},n-1)Xn−1=K(πn−1​,n−1) that gives Xn≃K(πn−1,n−1)×K(πn,n)X^n\simeq K(\pi_{n-1},n-1)\times K(\pi_n,n)Xn≃K(πn−1​,n−1)×K(πn​,n).

Remark 13.13 (Manipulating the two truncations). Postnikov stages cut the homotopy groups off above a degree; connected covers cut them off below. Both are determined by universal properties — a map from XXX inducing isomorphisms in a range, respectively a map to XXX doing so — and both are unique up to homotopy under or over XXX by Theorem 13.9. Two such constructions applied in the two possible orders therefore have the same homotopy groups, namely those of XXX in a window and zero outside it; and by uniqueness each is characterised by a universal property that the other also satisfies.

Writing that argument out is the natural exercise here, and it is a good test of whether Warning 13.2 has been absorbed: isomorphic homotopy groups are not enough, so the argument must produce a comparison map and then invoke Theorem 13.1. The uniqueness clause of Theorem 13.9 is what supplies it.

13.4 What the invariants miss

Remark 13.14 (Spaces). Warning 13.2 is worth restating as a summary of the book so far. For L(5,1)L(5,1)L(5,1) and L(5,2)L(5,2)L(5,2):

  • the fundamental groups agree (Example 2.21);

  • the homology groups agree (Example 4.8);

  • the cohomology rings agree (Example 7.10);

  • all higher homotopy groups agree (Warning 13.2);

and the spaces are still different. Every invariant built so far is a functor applied to one space at a time; what distinguishes these two is how their cells are linked, which is visible only to an operation that mixes degrees. Chapter 18 constructs the first such operation.

Remark 13.15 (Maps). There is a second failure, one degree more subtle, and it concerns maps rather than spaces. A map f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y induces f∗f_*f∗​ on homotopy groups and on homology. Both can vanish identically with fff still essential.

The place to look for such an fff is a two-stage Postnikov tower. If dim⁡X≤n\dim X\le ndimX≤n then [X,Y]→[X,Yn][X,Y]\to[X,Y^{n}][X,Y]→[X,Yn] is a bijection — by Exercise 11.8 and the extension lemma, nothing above stage nnn can be detected by a complex of dimension nnn — so classifying maps out of XXX reduces to classifying maps into a tower with two stages. Such a map is a cohomology class on XXX together with a lift, the obstruction to lifting is the kkk-invariant evaluated on that class, and the ambiguity in the lift is a quotient of a cohomology group of XXX. When the kkk-invariant is a cup square, as in Example 13.11, that obstruction is a quadratic condition, and quadratic conditions have solutions that linear invariants cannot see.

Carrying this out on a specific low-dimensional XXX is the natural place to test the chapter. Chapter 17 develops the machinery properly; what should be clear already is why π∗\pi_*π∗​ and H∗H_*H∗​ cannot be expected to suffice.

13.5 What to carry forward

  • Whitehead’s theorem needs a map (Theorem 13.1); Warning 13.2 is the standing counterexample to forgetting it.

  • The homological form needs simple connectivity (Theorem 13.4), and the Poincaré homology sphere shows why (Warning 13.5).

  • Work on the universal cover (Proposition 13.6); the right invariant is H∗(X~)H_*(\widetilde X)H∗​(X) as a Z[π1X]\ZZ[\pi_1X]Z[π1​X]-module (Remark 13.8) — the repair promised in Chapter 5.

  • Postnikov towers and kkk-invariants (Theorem 13.9); the kkk-invariant measures non-splitting (Exercise 13.12), and a cup square as kkk-invariant is the Hopf map in disguise (Example 13.11).

  • Neither π∗\pi_*π∗​ nor H∗H_*H∗​ classifies spaces (Remark 13.14) or maps (Remark 13.15).
