The cohomology Serre spectral sequence carries a multiplication, and that changes what it can do. Degree bookkeeping now constrains not only which groups appear but which products are possible, and a single differential propagates through a whole ring by the Leibniz rule. Most of the classical computations in this subject are one differential plus multiplicativity.

The chapter then extracts three named tools from the machine — Leray–Hirsch, Gysin, Thom — and ends with the invariant that measures how far a map S2n−1→SnS^{2n-1}\to S^nS2n−1→Sn is from being inessential.

16.2 Leray–Hirsch

Theorem 16.4 (Leray–Hirsch; recalled). Let F→E→pBF\to E\xrightarrow{p}BF→Ep​B be a fibration with BBB path-connected, and suppose each Hk(F;R)H^k(F;R)Hk(F;R) is a finitely generated free RRR-module. H∗(F;R)⊗RH∗(B;R)⟶H∗(E;R),i∗cj⊗b⟼cj⌣p∗b,H^*(F;R)\otimes_RH^*(B;R)\longrightarrow H^*(E;R),\qquad i^*c_j\otimes b\longmapsto c_j\smile p^*b,H∗(F;R)⊗R​H∗(B;R)⟶H∗(E;R),i∗cj​⊗b⟼cj​⌣p∗b,

is an isomorphism of H∗(B;R)H^*(B;R)H∗(B;R)-modules.

Warning 16.5. This is an isomorphism of modules, not of rings. It says nothing about the products cicjc_ic_jci​cj​, which have to be computed separately and which are exactly where the interesting information sits. A bundle whose Leray–Hirsch decomposition looks like a product can have a ring structure that is not the tensor product, and comparing two bundles with the same module structure and different rings is a standard way to produce spaces with identical cohomology groups and different homotopy type.

16.3 Gysin and Thom

A sphere bundle has a fibre with cohomology in only two degrees, so its spectral sequence has two rows and collapses into a long exact sequence.

Remark 16.17 (What the three tools do together). The tools of this chapter interlock in a way worth naming, because the combination is the standard route to the hardest classical results.

Suppose one is given a fibre bundle whose fibre, total space and base are all spheres. Theorem 16.9 constrains the dimensions immediately: the sequence relates the cohomology of the base to that of the total space through cup product with the Euler class, and a sphere has cohomology in only two degrees, so most of the maps are forced to be zero or isomorphisms and the possible dimensions are pinned down by arithmetic alone.

Then Theorem 16.12 converts the geometry into the Hopf invariant: the mapping cone of the bundle projection is the Thom space of the associated vector bundle, so the Thom class and the Euler class identify the cup square of Definition 16.13 with a characteristic class of the bundle. That identification turns “which sphere bundles over spheres exist” into “which Hopf invariants occur”.

At which point the answer is not elementary: Adams proved that H(f)=±1H(f)=\pm1H(f)=±1 is possible only for n=1,2,4,8n=1,2,4,8n=1,2,4,8. The book stops there and says so. What it does supply is everything up to that boundary — and Example 16.14 shows the boundary is attained.

16.5 What to carry forward

  • The spectral sequence is a ring and its differentials are derivations (Theorem 16.1); one differential plus Leibniz computes a whole ring (Example 16.2 and Exercise 16.3).

  • Leray–Hirsch (Theorem 16.4) gives a module isomorphism and never a ring isomorphism (Warning 16.5); what it assumes is that the fibre row consists of permanent cycles (Exercise 16.6).

  • The projective bundle formula (Example 16.7) is where Chern classes come from.

  • Gysin is the two-row spectral sequence, with the Euler class as the transgression (Exercise 16.10); Thom converts bundles into cones (Theorem 16.12).

  • The Hopf invariant (Definition 16.13) is unstable (Exercise 16.15) and vanishes in odd degrees (Exercise 16.16); it equals ±1\pm1±1 for the Hopf map (Example 16.14).
