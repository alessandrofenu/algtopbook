Three times now the book has stopped at the same wall. Chapter 4 found that homology cannot separate lens spaces; Chapter 7 found that neither can the cohomology ring, and promised an operation that is not induced by any multiplication; Chapter 13 added that even all the homotopy groups together fail on the same pair.

This chapter builds the missing invariant. The construction is in two steps: first, a formal identification of all natural transformations of cohomology with the cohomology of an Eilenberg–MacLane space — which converts a classification problem into a computation; second, the specific family of operations that fall out for mod-222 coefficients. Section 18.3 then uses them to separate two spaces whose cohomology rings agree over every coefficient ring at once.

18.1 Operations, and why they are computable

Definition 18.1. A cohomology operation of type (m,π;n,G)(m,\pi;n,G)(m,π;n,G) is a family of functions

θX ⁣:Hm(X;π)⟶Hn(X;G),\theta_X\colon H^m(X;\pi)\longrightarrow H^n(X;G),θX​:Hm(X;π)⟶Hn(X;G),

18.2 The Steenrod squares

Theorem 18.6 (Steenrod squares; recalled). There are cohomology operations

Sq⁡i ⁣:Hn(X;F2)⟶Hn+i(X;F2),i≥0,\Sq^i\colon H^n(X;\FF_2)\longrightarrow H^{n+i}(X;\FF_2),\qquad i\ge0,Sqi:Hn(X;F2​)⟶Hn+i(X;F2​),i≥0,

which are homomorphisms and satisfy:

  1. Sq⁡0=id\Sq^0=\mathrm{id}Sq0=id;

  2. Sq⁡n(x)=x2\Sq^n(x)=x^2Sqn(x)=x2 if ∣x∣=n|x|=n∣x∣=n, and Sq⁡i(x)=0\Sq^i(x)=0Sqi(x)=0 if i>∣x∣i>|x|i>∣x∣;

  3. the Cartan formula Sq⁡k(xy)=∑i+j=kSq⁡i(x) Sq⁡j(y)\Sq^k(xy)=\sum_{i+j=k}\Sq^i(x)\,\Sq^j(y)Sqk(xy)=∑i+j=k​Sqi(x)Sqj(y);

  4. stability: Sq⁡i\Sq^iSqi commutes with the suspension isomorphism

    H~n(X;F2) ≅ H~n+1(ΣX;F2).\widetilde H^n(X;\FF_2)\ \cong\ \widetilde H^{n+1}(\Sigma X;\FF_2).Hn(X;F2​) ≅ Hn+1(ΣX;F2​).

Axiom (b) is the source of everything: it says that in the top degree the operation is the cup square, and axiom (d) then propagates that square into degrees where no square exists.

Example 18.7 (The smallest computation). In H∗(CP2;F2)=F2[x]/(x3)H^*(\CP^2;\FF_2)=\FF_2[x]/(x^3)H∗(CP2;F2​)=F2​[x]/(x3) with ∣x∣=2|x|=2∣x∣=2, axiom (b) gives

Sq⁡2x=x2≠0.\Sq^2x=x^2\neq0 .Sq2x=x2=0.

There is nothing else to compute: Sq⁡0x=x\Sq^0x=xSq0x=x, Sq⁡1x\Sq^1xSq1x lies in H3(CP2)=0H^3(\CP^2)=0H3(CP2)=0, and Sq⁡ix=0\Sq^ix=0Sqix=0 for i>2i>2i>2.

Warning 18.8 (Stability is what makes them useful). Axiom (d) is the axiom that does the work in Section 18.3, and it is worth seeing why. On a suspension every cup product of positive-degree classes vanishes (Exercise 16.15), so the cohomology ring of a suspension carries no information beyond its groups. But the Steenrod operations survive suspension unchanged. So on suspensions — where the ring is guaranteed to be useless — the operations are exactly as informative as they were before.

18.3 Where the ring is not enough

Theorem 18.9. Over every commutative coefficient ring, ΣCP2\Sigma\CP^2ΣCP2 and S3∨S5S^3\vee S^5S3∨S5 have isomorphic cohomology rings. They are nevertheless not homotopy equivalent.

Proof. The rings agree. H~∗(CP2;R)\widetilde H^*(\CP^2;R)H∗(CP2;R) is RRR in degrees 222 and 444, so H~∗(ΣCP2;R)\widetilde H^*(\Sigma\CP^2;R)H∗(ΣCP2;R) is RRR in degrees 333 and 555; and H~∗(S3∨S5;R)\widetilde H^*(S^3\vee S^5;R)H∗(S3∨S5;R) is the same. In both cases every product of positive-degree classes vanishes: on ΣCP2\Sigma\CP^2ΣCP2 because it is a suspension (Exercise 16.15), and on S3∨S5S^3\vee S^5S3∨S5 because the classes come from different wedge summands and each squares into a zero group (Exercise 7.3). Two graded rings which agree additively and have identically zero products are isomorphic.

The spaces differ. Work mod 222. Write σ\sigmaσ for the suspension isomorphism and let xxx generate H2(CP2;F2)H^2(\CP^2;\FF_2)H2(CP2;F2​). By stability and Example 18.7,

Sq⁡2(σx)=σ(Sq⁡2x)=σ(x2)≠0in H5(ΣCP2;F2),\Sq^2(\sigma x)=\sigma\big(\Sq^2x\big)=\sigma\big(x^2\big)\neq0 \quad\text{in } H^5(\Sigma\CP^2;\FF_2),Sq2(σx)=σ(Sq2x)=σ(x2)=0in H5(ΣCP2;F2​),

since σ\sigmaσ is an isomorphism and x2x^2x2 generates H4(CP2;F2)H^4(\CP^2;\FF_2)H4(CP2;F2​).

On S3∨S5S^3\vee S^5S3∨S5, let aaa generate H3H^3H3. Restricting to the two summands is injective on H~5\widetilde H^5H5, and Sq⁡2\Sq^2Sq2 is natural, so it suffices to compute Sq⁡2\Sq^2Sq2 of the restrictions. On S3S^3S3 the class restricts to the generator of H3(S3;F2)H^3(S^3;\FF_2)H3(S3;F2​) and Sq⁡2\Sq^2Sq2 of it lies in H5(S3;F2)=0H^5(S^3;\FF_2)=0H5(S3;F2​)=0; on S5S^5S5 the restriction of aaa is 000. A homotopy equivalence would induce an isomorphism of mod-222 cohomology commuting with Sq⁡2\Sq^2Sq2, carrying a generator of H3H^3H3 to a generator of H3H^3H3. On one side Sq⁡2\Sq^2Sq2 of such a generator is nonzero and on the other it is zero, so no such isomorphism exists.□

Remark 18.10 (The debt, paid). Theorem 18.9 is what Remark 7.12 promised: a pair of spaces with isomorphic cohomology rings, over every coefficient ring at once, that are not homotopy equivalent. The invariant separating them is natural in the space and commutes with induced maps — so it is an invariant of homotopy type — and it is not induced by any product, since both products are zero.

The structural reason is Warning 18.8. A suspension has no ring structure worth the name, and the Steenrod operations are precisely the part of the multiplicative structure that survives suspension: Sq⁡n\Sq^nSqn on a class of degree nnn is the cup square, and stability carries the information about that square into a space where the square itself is unavailable. The operation remembers a product that the ring has forgotten.

Remark 18.15 (Identifying Sq⁡1\Sq^1Sq1). Example 18.14 invites a comparison, and the ingredients for making it are now all available.

The group of stable operations Hq(−;F2)→Hq+1(−;F2)H^q(-;\FF_2)\to H^{q+1}(-;\FF_2)Hq(−;F2​)→Hq+1(−;F2​) is small: by Theorem 18.2 it is Hq+1(K(F2,q);F2)H^{q+1}(K(\FF_2,q);\FF_2)Hq+1(K(F2​,q);F2​), which is F2\FF_2F2​ for q≥1q\ge1q≥1. So there is exactly one nonzero stable operation of degree one, and any two ways of producing one must agree as soon as both are shown to be nonzero. Chapter 9 produced one — the Bockstein β\betaβ, which is natural, stable, and squares to zero by Exercise 9.12 — and this chapter produces another.

Checking that both are nonzero requires a single example, and Example 9.16 has already computed β\betaβ on H∗(RP∞;F2)H^*(\RP^\infty;\FF_2)H∗(RP∞;F2​). Carrying out the comparison, and being explicit about why one example suffices once uniqueness is known, is the natural exercise here; the trap is to verify agreement on one space and conclude agreement in general without the uniqueness statement.
