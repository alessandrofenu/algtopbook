Chapter 18 introduced the squares axiomatically and used one of them. This chapter treats A\mathcal{A}A as an algebra: it has a basis, a coproduct, and relations that constrain what its elements can do. The payoff is a theorem no earlier chapter could reach — a numerical restriction on which spheres can carry a Hopf invariant one map, proved by nothing more than the Adem relations and a degree count.

Remark 27.4. Example 27.3 was carried out on a class of degree two. Carrying out that iteration, and identifying which sequences of squares survive, is the natural exercise once Section 27.2 has been read.

Remark 27.11 (How far this gets, and what it costs). Theorem 27.10 narrows the possible dimensions from all nnn to n∈{1,2,4,8,16,… }n\in\{1,2,4,8,16,\dots\}n∈{1,2,4,8,16,…}, and it does so with the Adem relations and one degree count. The classical Hopf maps realise n=1,2,4,8n=1,2,4,8n=1,2,4,8 (Example 16.14 and its quaternionic and octonionic analogues), so the question is whether n=16n=16n=16 or beyond can occur.

They cannot, but that is Adams’ theorem, proved with secondary operations or with KKK-theory, and the book does not prove it — as Remark 16.17 already said. What is worth registering is how much of the way an elementary argument goes: everything in this chapter is a manipulation of the algebra A\mathcal{A}A, and it reduces an infinite family of possibilities to a logarithmically sparse one.

The same style of argument constrains division algebras, parallelisability of spheres, and the existence of certain fibre bundles — all of them, by Remark 16.17, questions about the same integer.

27.4 What to carry forward

  • The total square is a ring map (Exercise 27.2); every computation follows from that plus one value (Example 27.3).

  • The admissible basis and excess (Definition 27.5 and Theorem 27.6); Adem relations are used by rewriting, as in Exercise 27.7.

  • A\mathcal{A}A is a Hopf algebra generated by the Sq⁡2j\Sq^{2^j}Sq2j (Remark 27.8 and Proposition 27.9).

  • Odd Hopf invariant forces nnn to be a power of 222 (Theorem 27.10) — an elementary argument that reaches within one step of Adams’ theorem (Remark 27.11).
