Chapter 1

Basepoints, conjugacy, and the change-of-basepoint action

In this chapter

  1. 1.1 The change-of-basepoint isomorphism
  2. 1.2 Free versus based homotopy
  3. 1.3 Homotopies of maps, and the centre
  4. 1.4 What to carry forward

Every first course proves that a path from x0x_0x0​ to x1x_1x1​ induces an isomorphism π1(X,x1)→π1(X,x0)\pi_1(X,x_1)\to\pi_1(X,x_0)π1​(X,x1​)→π1​(X,x0​), and then quietly starts writing π1(X)\pi_1(X)π1​(X). This chapter is about the word quietly. The isomorphism is not canonical: it depends on the path, and two paths differ by conjugation by a loop. That ambiguity is not a blemish to be removed. It is the first appearance of a structure that organises the whole subject — in Chapter 3 it becomes the statement that conjugate subgroups classify isomorphic covering spaces, and in Chapter 15 it returns as the monodromy action attached to a fibration.

Throughout the book we use the standard results of a first course without comment: π1(S1)≅Z\pi_1(S^1)\cong\ZZπ1​(S1)≅Z, the van Kampen theorem, the existence and uniqueness of lifts against a covering space. What such a course tends to leave as folklore — exactly which isomorphisms are canonical, and what happens to a basepoint during a homotopy — is what we set down first, because every later chapter leans on it.

Conventions. A path is a map γ ⁣:I→X\gamma\colon I\to Xγ:I→X; we write γ(0)\gamma(0)γ(0) for its initial and γ(1)\gamma(1)γ(1) for its terminal point. The concatenation γ⋅δ\gamma\cdot\deltaγ⋅δ, defined when γ(1)=δ(0)\gamma(1)=\delta(0)γ(1)=δ(0), traverses γ\gammaγ first. The reverse path is γˉ(s)=γ(1−s)\bar\gamma(s)=\gamma(1-s)γˉ​(s)=γ(1−s), and cxc_xcx​ is the constant path at xxx. Homotopies of paths are always rel {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1} unless we say otherwise; homotopies of maps never are.

1.1 The change-of-basepoint isomorphism

Definition 1.1. Let γ ⁣:I→X\gamma\colon I\to Xγ:I→X satisfy γ(0)=x0\gamma(0)=x_0γ(0)=x0​ and γ(1)=x1\gamma(1)=x_1γ(1)=x1​. Define

βγ ⁣:π1(X,x1)⟶π1(X,x0),βγ[α]=[γ⋅α⋅γˉ].\beta_\gamma\colon\pi_1(X,x_1)\longrightarrow\pi_1(X,x_0), \qquad \beta_\gamma[\alpha]=[\gamma\cdot\alpha\cdot\bar\gamma].βγ​:π1​(X,x1​)⟶π1​(X,x0​),βγ​[α]=[γ⋅α⋅γˉ​].

Note the direction: βγ\beta_\gammaβγ​ runs backwards along γ\gammaγ, from the group at the far end to the group at the near end. Getting this consistently right is worth a minute now and saves an hour later.

Proposition 1.2. βγ\beta_\gammaβγ​ is a well-defined group isomorphism, and:

  1. βγ\beta_\gammaβγ​ depends only on the homotopy class of γ\gammaγ rel {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1};

  2. βγ⋅δ=βγ∘βδ\beta_{\gamma\cdot\delta}=\beta_\gamma\circ\beta_\deltaβγ⋅δ​=βγ​∘βδ​ whenever the concatenation is defined;

  3. βcx0=id\beta_{c_{x_0}}=\mathrm{id}βcx0​​​=id, and βγ−1=βγˉ\beta_\gamma^{-1}=\beta_{\bar\gamma}βγ−1​=βγˉ​​.

Proof. Well-definedness and the homomorphism property are the usual computations: a homotopy αt\alpha_tαt​ rel endpoints yields the homotopy γ⋅αt⋅γˉ\gamma\cdot\alpha_t\cdot\bar\gammaγ⋅αt​⋅γˉ​, and (γαγˉ)(γβγˉ)≃γ(αβ)γˉ(\gamma\alpha\bar\gamma)(\gamma\beta\bar\gamma)\simeq\gamma(\alpha\beta)\bar\gamma(γαγˉ​)(γβγˉ​)≃γ(αβ)γˉ​ because γˉ⋅γ≃cx1\bar\gamma\cdot\gamma\simeq c_{x_1}γˉ​⋅γ≃cx1​​. Part (c) is immediate, and with (b) it shows βγ\beta_\gammaβγ​ is invertible. Part (a) is Exercise 1.3.□

βγ[α]=[γ][α][γ]−1for all [α]∈π1(X,x0).\beta_\gamma[\alpha]=[\gamma][\alpha][\gamma]^{-1} \qquad\text{for all }[\alpha]\in\pi_1(X,x_0).βγ​[α]=[γ][α][γ]−1for all [α]∈π1​(X,x0​).

Example 1.5. Let X=S1∨S1X=S^1\vee S^1X=S1∨S1 with π1(X,x0)=F(a,b)\pi_1(X,x_0)=F(a,b)π1​(X,x0​)=F(a,b) free on the two circles. Corollary 1.6. Let XXX be path-connected. The isomorphism π1(X,x1)≅π1(X,x0)\pi_1(X,x_1)\cong\pi_1(X,x_0)π1​(X,x1​)≅π1​(X,x0​) is independent of the choice of path for every pair x0,x1x_0,x_1x0​,x1​ if and only if π1(X,x0)\pi_1(X,x_0)π1​(X,x0​) is abelian.

Proof. Two paths γ,δ\gamma,\deltaγ,δ from x0x_0x0​ to x1x_1x1​ give βγ∘βδ−1=βγ∘βδˉ=βγ⋅δˉ\beta_\gamma\circ\beta_\delta^{-1}=\beta_\gamma\circ\beta_{\bar\delta} =\beta_{\gamma\cdot\bar\delta}βγ​∘βδ−1​=βγ​∘βδˉ​=βγ⋅δˉ​ by Proposition 1.2, and γ⋅δˉ\gamma\cdot\bar\deltaγ⋅δˉ is a loop at x0x_0x0​. By Lemma 1.4 this is conjugation by [γδˉ][\gamma\bar\delta][γδˉ]. So all choices agree exactly when every loop at x0x_0x0​ acts trivially by conjugation, i.e. when the group is abelian; and conversely, every class arises as some [γδˉ][\gamma\bar\delta][γδˉ] (take δ\deltaδ fixed and γ=λ⋅δ\gamma=\lambda\cdot\deltaγ=λ⋅δ for a loop λ\lambdaλ).□

Remark 1.7. This is the precise content of the abuse of notation π1(X)\pi_1(X)π1​(X): for path-connected XXX the fundamental group is well defined up to isomorphism, and canonically well defined only up to inner automorphism. Any statement one makes about π1(X)\pi_1(X)π1​(X) without naming a basepoint must therefore be invariant under inner automorphisms — being abelian, being finite, having a given conjugacy class of subgroups. Being equal to a particular subgroup is not such a statement, which is exactly why Chapter 3 classifies covering spaces by conjugacy classes of subgroups and not by subgroups.

1.2 Free versus based homotopy

A loop at x0x_0x0​ is a map (S1,s0)→(X,x0)(S^1,s_0)\to(X,x_0)(S1,s0​)→(X,x0​); two such may be compared with or without keeping the basepoint fixed. The two notions differ, and the difference is measured by exactly the maps of the previous section.

Definition 1.9. Loops f,gf,gf,g in XXX are freely homotopic if they are homotopic as maps S1→XS^1\to XS1→X, with no condition on basepoints. They are based homotopic (at x0x_0x0​) if f(s0)=g(s0)=x0f(s_0)=g(s_0)=x_0f(s0​)=g(s0​)=x0​ and the homotopy may be taken to fix s0s_0s0​ throughout, i.e. if [f]=[g][f]=[g][f]=[g] in π1(X,x0)\pi_1(X,x_0)π1​(X,x0​).

One direction is a construction, and it is worth doing explicitly because the same picture — shrinking a path away — reappears whenever a basepoint has to be moved.

Lemma 1.10. Let hhh be a path from x0x_0x0​ to x1x_1x1​ and let α\alphaα be a loop at x1x_1x1​. Then the loop h⋅α⋅hˉh\cdot\alpha\cdot\bar hh⋅α⋅hˉ at x0x_0x0​ is freely homotopic to α\alphaα.

Proof. For t∈It\in It∈I let ht(s)=h(t+(1−t)s)h_t(s)=h\big(t+(1-t)s\big)ht​(s)=h(t+(1−t)s), the tail of hhh from h(t)h(t)h(t) to x1x_1x1​, and set φt=ht⋅α⋅hˉt\varphi_t=h_t\cdot\alpha\cdot\bar h_tφt​=ht​⋅α⋅hˉt​, a loop based at h(t)h(t)h(t). The map (s,t)↦φt(s)(s,t)\mapsto\varphi_t(s)(s,t)↦φt​(s) is continuous, φ0=h⋅α⋅hˉ\varphi_0=h\cdot\alpha\cdot\bar hφ0​=h⋅α⋅hˉ, and φ1=cx1⋅α⋅cx1\varphi_1=c_{x_1}\cdot\alpha\cdot c_{x_1}φ1​=cx1​​⋅α⋅cx1​​, which is α\alphaα up to reparametrisation. This is a homotopy of maps S1→XS^1\to XS1→X; its basepoint travels along hhh, which is precisely what a free homotopy is allowed to do.□

The other direction — what a free homotopy does to a based class — follows from a lemma about homotopies of arbitrary maps, which we will use far more often than the loop case.

φ0∗=βh∘φ1∗ ⁣:π1(X,x0)⟶π1(Y,φ0(x0)).\varphi_{0*}=\beta_h\circ\varphi_{1*} \colon\pi_1(X,x_0)\longrightarrow\pi_1\big(Y,\varphi_0(x_0)\big).φ0∗​=βh​∘φ1∗​:π1​(X,x0​)⟶π1​(Y,φ0​(x0​)).

Remark 1.12. Two features of Lemma 1.11 are worth naming, because both get used. First, it is a statement about any homotopy, not only one between maps that agree somewhere. Example 1.13. Let X=Y=S1X=Y=S^1X=Y=S1 and let φt\varphi_tφt​ be rotation by 2πt2\pi t2πt, so φ0=φ1=id\varphi_0=\varphi_1 =\mathrm{id}φ0​=φ1​=id. The first serious payoff of Lemma 1.11 is that homotopy equivalences behave as one wants them to, even though a homotopy inverse cannot usually be chosen to respect basepoints.

1.3 Homotopies of maps, and the centre

Lemma 1.4 says that βγ\beta_\gammaβγ​ is the identity exactly when [γ][\gamma][γ] is central. So any construction that forces βγ\beta_\gammaβγ​ to be the identity produces central elements. That is a small observation with a long reach: it is the mechanism behind the fact that H-spaces have abelian fundamental group, and — one step further, which we leave for the reader to take when they meet it — behind the constraint on loops of self-maps.

Definition 1.18. An H-space is a space XXX with a basepoint eee and a map μ ⁣:X×X→X\mu\colon X\times X\to Xμ:X×X→X satisfying μ(e,e)=e\mu(e,e)=eμ(e,e)=e, such that μ(e,−)\mu(e,-)μ(e,−) and μ(−,e)\mu(-,e)μ(−,e) are homotopic to idX\mathrm{id}_XidX​. It has a strict unit if μ(e,x)=μ(x,e)=x\mu(e,x)=\mu(x,e)=xμ(e,x)=μ(x,e)=x for all xxx; topological groups are the standard example.

Proposition 1.19 (Eckmann–Hilton). Let XXX be an H-space with a strict unit eee. Then π1(X,e)\pi_1(X,e)π1​(X,e) is abelian.

Proof. Besides concatenation ∗*∗, the set π1(X,e)\pi_1(X,e)π1​(X,e) carries a second operation: [f]⊙[g]=[μ∘(f,g)][f]\odot[g]=[\mu\circ(f,g)][f]⊙[g]=[μ∘(f,g)], the pointwise product, which is well defined because μ\muμ takes homotopies to homotopies. 1.4 What to carry forward

  • βγ\beta_\gammaβγ​ (Definition 1.1) and its four formal properties (Proposition 1.2); for a loop it is conjugation (Lemma 1.4).

  • The tracking lemma (Lemma 1.11): a homotopy that moves the basepoint changes the induced map by βh\beta_hβh​, and the correction is explicit. This is used again in Chapter 11 for homotopy extension arguments and in Chapter 13 for maps between towers.

  • Homotopy equivalences induce isomorphisms on π1\pi_1π1​ (Proposition 1.14) — the reason we may compute with any convenient model of a space.

  • Free and based homotopy of loops differ exactly by conjugation, in the two directions supplied by Lemma 1.10 and Lemma 1.11.

The ambiguity isolated here is about to become an organising principle rather than a nuisance.
