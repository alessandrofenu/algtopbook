Chapter 17

Obstruction theory and Postnikov towers, operationally

In this chapter

  1. 17.1 The obstruction cocycle
  2. 17.2 Lifting

  4. 17.4 What obstruction theory is for
  5. 17.5 What to carry forward

Theorem 11.11 extended a map over a CW pair by assuming that every obstruction vanished for want of anywhere to live. This chapter keeps the obstructions instead of assuming them away, and organises them into cohomology classes. The result is a machine that answers questions of the form “does this map extend?”, “does this bundle have a section?”, “does this map lift?” with a computable class rather than a hope.

Two habits carry over from Chapter 15. The obstruction is defined cell by cell but only its cohomology class is meaningful, and the passage from cocycle to class is exactly the freedom to modify what has already been built. And the whole theory is stated for a target whose fundamental group acts trivially on its higher homotopy; without that, everything below needs local coefficients.

17.1 The obstruction cocycle

Let (W,A)(W,A)(W,A) be a CW pair and YYY a simple space — one where π1(Y)\pi_1(Y)π1​(Y) acts trivially on every πn(Y)\pi_n(Y)πn​(Y), so that πn(Y)\pi_n(Y)πn​(Y) needs no basepoint.

Construction 17.1. Suppose fff is defined on A∪WnA\cup W^{n}A∪Wn. For each (n+1)(n+1)(n+1)-cell eee of WWW with attaching map φe ⁣:Sn→Wn\varphi_e\colon S^{n}\to W^{n}φe​:Sn→Wn, the composite f∘φef\circ\varphi_ef∘φe​ is a map Sn→YS^{n}\to YSn→Y; let

c(f)(e)=[f∘φe]∈πn(Y).c(f)(e)=\big[f\circ\varphi_e\big]\in\pi_{n}(Y).c(f)(e)=[f∘φe​]∈πn​(Y).

This defines a cellular cochain c(f)∈Cn+1(W,A;πnY)c(f)\in C^{n+1}\big(W,A;\pi_{n}Y\big)c(f)∈Cn+1(W,A;πn​Y), the obstruction cochain.

Theorem 17.2 (Obstruction theory; recalled). With notation as above:

  1. c(f)c(f)c(f) is a cocycle, and it vanishes identically if and only if fff extends over A∪Wn+1A\cup W^{n+1}A∪Wn+1;

  2. its class [c(f)]∈Hn+1(W,A;πnY)[c(f)]\in H^{n+1}\big(W,A;\pi_{n}Y\big)[c(f)]∈Hn+1(W,A;πn​Y) vanishes if and only if the restriction of fff to A∪Wn−1A\cup W^{n-1}A∪Wn−1 extends over A∪Wn+1A\cup W^{n+1}A∪Wn+1 — that is, if and only if fff can be altered on the nnn-cells so as to extend.

Warning 17.3 (Cochain versus class). The two statements differ, and the difference is the whole point. The cochain c(f)c(f)c(f) answers “does this fff extend?”; its class answers “does some map agreeing with fff away from the nnn-cells extend?”. Only the second is a cohomological invariant, and only the second is stable under the modifications one always wants to make. A reader who computes c(f)≠0c(f)\ne0c(f)=0 and concludes that no extension exists has proved nothing.

Example 17.4 (One cell). If W=A∪en+1W=A\cup e^{n+1}W=A∪en+1 is a single cell attached along φ\varphiφ, then Hn+1(W,A;πnY)≅πn(Y)H^{n+1}(W,A;\pi_nY)\cong\pi_n(Y)Hn+1(W,A;πn​Y)≅πn​(Y) and the obstruction is literally [f∘φ][f\circ\varphi][f∘φ]. So fff extends over the cell precisely when the composite Sn→A→YS^n\to A\to YSn→A→Y is nullhomotopic, and a nullhomotopy is the extension. This is the entire content of Theorem 17.2 in the smallest case, and it was the inductive step in the proof of Theorem 11.11.

Exercise 17.5 (easy). Deduce Theorem 11.11 from Theorem 17.2.

Solution. Suppose the vanishing hypothesis of Theorem 11.11 holds, so that πk−1(Y)\pi_{k-1}(Y)πk−1​(Y) is trivial in every dimension kkk occupied by a relative cell. Then the coefficient group πn(Y)\pi_{n}(Y)πn​(Y) appearing in Theorem 17.2 is zero in each relevant degree, hence Hn+1(W,A;πnY)=0H^{n+1}(W,A;\pi_nY)=0Hn+1(W,A;πn​Y)=0 and every obstruction class vanishes. Extending skeleton by skeleton, the map extends over all of WWW.

Exercise 17.6 (medium). Let Y=K(π,n)Y=K(\pi,n)Y=K(π,n) and let (W,A)(W,A)(W,A) be a CW pair. Show that a map A→K(π,n)A\to K(\pi,n)A→K(π,n) extends over WWW if and only if its class in Hn(A;π)H^n(A;\pi)Hn(A;π) lies in the image of the restriction Hn(W;π)→Hn(A;π)H^n(W;\pi)\to H^n(A;\pi)Hn(W;π)→Hn(A;π).

Hint. Use the identification [X,K(π,n)]≅Hn(X;π)[X,K(\pi,n)]\cong H^n(X;\pi)[X,K(π,n)]≅Hn(X;π) and the naturality of that bijection.

Solution. Under the natural bijection [X,K(π,n)]≅Hn(X;π)[X,K(\pi,n)]\cong H^n(X;\pi)[X,K(π,n)]≅Hn(X;π), a map A→K(π,n)A\to K(\pi,n)A→K(π,n) corresponds to a class α∈Hn(A;π)\alpha\in H^n(A;\pi)α∈Hn(A;π), and extending it over WWW corresponds to finding α~∈Hn(W;π)\tilde\alpha\in H^n(W;\pi)α~∈Hn(W;π) restricting to α\alphaα, since the bijection is natural in the space. Hence extendability is exactly membership in the image of the restriction map.

Consistently with Theorem 17.2: πk(K(π,n))\pi_k(K(\pi,n))πk​(K(π,n)) vanishes except for k=nk=nk=n, so there is only one possibly nonzero obstruction, and it is the primary one.

17.2 Lifting

The same argument runs against a fibration instead of a space.

Theorem 17.7 (Obstructions to a lift; recalled). Let F→X→pBF\to X\xrightarrow{p}BF→Xp​B be a fibration with FFF simple, let (W,A)(W,A)(W,A) be a CW pair, let g ⁣:W→Bg\colon W\to Bg:W→B, and suppose a lift of g∣Ag|_Ag∣A​ to XXX is given. The obstructions to extending that lift over WWW lie in the groups

Hn+1(W,A;πnF),n≥1,H^{n+1}\big(W,A;\pi_{n}F\big),\qquad n\ge1 ,Hn+1(W,A;πn​F),n≥1,

one for each skeleton, each defined once the previous ones have been arranged to vanish.

Example 17.8 (Sections in low dimensions). Take A=∅A=\varnothingA=∅ and g=idBg=\mathrm{id}_Bg=idB​, so that a lift is a section of p ⁣:X→Bp\colon X\to Bp:X→B. If dim⁡W≤n\dim W\le ndimW≤n and πi(F)=0\pi_i(F)=0πi​(F)=0 for i<ni<ni<n, every obstruction group Hi+1(W;πiF)H^{i+1}(W;\pi_iF)Hi+1(W;πi​F) vanishes — for i<ni<ni<n because the coefficients do, and for i≥ni\ge ni≥n because Hi+1(W)=0H^{i+1}(W)=0Hi+1(W)=0 by dimension. So a section exists. This is the standard reason that a fibration with highly connected fibre has sections over low-dimensional complexes.

Solution. Give W×IW\times IW×I the product CW structure. The subcomplex W×∂I∪A×IW\times\partial I\cup A\times IW×∂I∪A×I contains both ends and the part of the cylinder over AAA. The cells not in it are exactly the products e×(0,1)e\times(0,1)e×(0,1) for cells eee of W∖AW\setminus AW∖A, and dim⁡(e×(0,1))=dim⁡e+1\dim\big(e\times(0,1)\big)=\dim e+1dim(e×(0,1))=dime+1.

Remark 17.10 (Existence versus uniqueness). Every existence statement in this subject has a uniqueness companion obtained by running the same argument on a cylinder. Given two solutions of a lifting problem that agree on AAA, a homotopy rel AAA between them is a lift over the pair of Exercise 17.9; and by that exercise its relative cells sit one dimension higher than those of (W,A)(W,A)(W,A). Feeding this into Theorem 17.7 therefore produces obstruction groups shifted by one, with the coefficients unchanged.

Carrying that out — and being careful about which direction the shift goes, so that the answer lands in Hn(W,A;πnF)H^{n}(W,A;\pi_nF)Hn(W,A;πn​F) rather than Hn+2H^{n+2}Hn+2 — is the natural exercise here. It is worth doing once explicitly, because the shifted statement is used far more often than the unshifted one: in practice one rarely asks whether a lift exists and almost always whether two given ones agree.

17.3 Principal fibrations

Definition 17.11. A fibration E→BE\to BE→B is principal if it is the pullback of a path–loop fibration: there is a map k ⁣:B→Ck\colon B\to Ck:B→C and a homotopy equivalence of fibrations

E≃B×CPC,E\simeq B\times_{C}PC ,E≃B×C​PC,

where PC→CPC\to CPC→C is the path fibration. The fibre is then ΩC\Omega CΩC.

Example 17.12. By Theorem 13.9 each stage Xn→Xn−1X^n\to X^{n-1}Xn→Xn−1 of a Postnikov tower is pulled back from the path fibration over K(πnX,n+1)K(\pi_nX,n+1)K(πn​X,n+1) along the kkk-invariant. So every Postnikov stage is principal, essentially by construction, and the fibre K(πnX,n)K(\pi_nX,n)K(πn​X,n) is ΩK(πnX,n+1)\Omega K(\pi_nX,n+1)ΩK(πn​X,n+1) as Definition 17.11 requires.

Warning 17.13. Principal does not mean trivial. A principal fibration is a pullback of a universal fibration, not a product; it is trivial exactly when the classifying map kkk is nullhomotopic (Exercise 13.12). Nor does principal mean that the tower splits: each stage being principal says nothing about how the stages compose.

Exercise 17.14 (easy). Show that the loop functor preserves pullbacks: for maps B→C←DB\to C\leftarrow DB→C←D,

Ω(B×CD)≅ΩB×ΩCΩD.\Omega\big(B\times_CD\big)\cong\Omega B\times_{\Omega C}\Omega D .Ω(B×C​D)≅ΩB×ΩC​ΩD.

Hint. ΩX=Map∗(S1,X)\Omega X=\mathrm{Map}_*(S^1,X)ΩX=Map∗​(S1,X), and Map∗(S1,−)\mathrm{Map}_*(S^1,-)Map∗​(S1,−) is a right adjoint.

Solution. Ω=Map∗(S1,−)\Omega=\mathrm{Map}_*(S^1,-)Ω=Map∗​(S1,−) is right adjoint to S1∧−S^1\wedge-S1∧−, and right adjoints preserve limits; a pullback is a limit. Concretely, a loop in B×CDB\times_CDB×C​D is a pair of loops in BBB and DDD agreeing after mapping to CCC, which is exactly a point of ΩB×ΩCΩD\Omega B\times_{\Omega C}\Omega DΩB×ΩC​ΩD.

Remark 17.16 (What looping does to a tower). Put the two exercises together. Applying Ω\OmegaΩ to the pullback square that exhibits a Postnikov stage as principal produces another pullback square (Exercise 17.14), and the universal fibration at the corner is carried to the universal fibration one degree down (Exercise 17.15). The looped tower is therefore again a tower of the same shape, with the kkk-invariants replaced by their loopings.

Two features of the looped tower are worth anticipating. Its stages are principal for a structural reason rather than by construction — which matters, because Example 17.12 was an artefact of how the tower was built and says nothing about towers arising otherwise. And Ωk\Omega kΩk is a loop map, hence an H-map, so the kkk-invariants of the looped tower respect the multiplications of Chapter 10. Working out precisely what survives is the natural exercise, and both ingredients are on this page.

17.4 What obstruction theory is for

Remark 17.17. Three uses, all of which the book meets again.

Classifying maps. Remark 13.15 observed that a map into a two-stage tower is a cohomology class plus a lift, with the kkk-invariant as the obstruction and a cohomology group as the indeterminacy. Theorem 17.7 is what makes that precise, and Remark 17.10 is what counts the lifts once they exist. When the kkk-invariant is a cup square, as in Example 13.11, the obstruction is a quadratic function of the class — which is why linear invariants such as π∗\pi_*π∗​ and H∗H_*H∗​ cannot detect the answer.

Constructing operations. Exercise 17.6 turns maps into Eilenberg–MacLane spaces into cohomology classes. Chapter 18 inverts that: natural transformations of cohomology are computed by computing the cohomology of an Eilenberg–MacLane space.

Combinatorial lifting. The pattern “build over a skeleton, meet an obstruction in a homotopy group, kill it or record it” becomes, in Chapter 23, the horn-filling conditions defining Kan complexes and quasi-categories. There the obstruction groups disappear because the lifts are required to exist outright, and what remains is exactly the combinatorics.

17.5 What to carry forward

  • The obstruction cochain (Construction 17.1) and the difference between it and its class (Warning 17.3).

  • Obstructions to extending a map live in Hn+1(W,A;πnY)H^{n+1}(W,A;\pi_nY)Hn+1(W,A;πn​Y), and to lifting one in Hn+1(W,A;πnF)H^{n+1}(W,A;\pi_nF)Hn+1(W,A;πn​F) (Theorems 17.2 and 17.7). The extension lemma is the case in which they all vanish (Exercise 17.5).

  • Uniqueness is existence on a cylinder, with the degree shifted by one (Exercise 17.9 and Remark 17.10).

  • Postnikov stages are principal (Example 17.12), which is not the same as trivial (Warning 17.13); Ω\OmegaΩ preserves pullbacks and shifts Eilenberg–MacLane spaces (Exercises 17.14 and 17.15).
