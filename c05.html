An exact sequence is not a theorem to be admired but a device to be operated. In each of the three sections below the pattern is the same: a geometric decomposition produces a long exact sequence, and the sequence produces an answer by an entirely mechanical procedure — a cokernel at one end, a kernel at the other, and an extension problem in between. The skill being trained is running the machine confidently, and knowing which of its hypotheses is load-bearing.

The chapter closes by looking at what all this computation cannot see. Homology is about to be the third invariant of Part I to run out of power, and the way it fails — it cannot see the universal cover — points straight at Chapter 13.

5.1 Pairs, quotients, and a hypothesis that matters

The long exact sequence of a pair (X,A)(X,A)(X,A),

⋯→Hn(A)→Hn(X)→Hn(X,A)→ ∂ Hn−1(A)→⋯ ,\cdots\to H_n(A)\to H_n(X)\to H_n(X,A)\xrightarrow{\ \partial\ }H_{n-1}(A)\to\cdots,⋯→Hn​(A)→Hn​(X)→Hn​(X,A) ∂ ​Hn−1​(A)→⋯,

is useless without a way of identifying Hn(X,A)H_n(X,A)Hn​(X,A), and the identification one reaches for is Hn(X,A)≅H~n(X/A)H_n(X,A)\cong\widetilde H_n(X/A)Hn​(X,A)≅Hn​(X/A). That isomorphism is a theorem with a hypothesis.

Theorem 5.1 (Good pairs; recalled). If AAA is a nonempty closed subspace that is a deformation retract of some neighbourhood in XXX — for instance if (X,A)(X,A)(X,A) is a CW pair — then the quotient map induces an isomorphism Hn(X,A)≅H~n(X/A)H_n(X,A)\cong\widetilde H_n(X/A)Hn​(X,A)≅Hn​(X/A) for all nnn.

Hint. Work cell by cell: in a cell ene^nen attached to AAA, push away from the centre.

Solution. Build NNN skeleton by skeleton. Having a neighbourhood Nn−1N^{n-1}Nn−1 of A∩Xn−1A\cap X^{n-1}A∩Xn−1 in Xn−1X^{n-1}Xn−1 with a deformation retraction to it, extend over each nnn-cell: in the characteristic map Dn→XD^n\to XDn→X, take the preimage of Nn−1N^{n-1}Nn−1 in ∂Dn\partial D^n∂Dn, thicken it radially inwards to an open collar, and deformation retract that collar radially. Two facts about the failure of Theorem 5.1 are worth having, because both are counterintuitive and both are used to build counterexamples.

Example 5.3 (Zero-dimensional homology counts components, one at a time). If AAA is a space with infinitely many path components, then H0(A)≅⨁π0(A)ZH_0(A)\cong\bigoplus_{\pi_0(A)}\ZZH0​(A)≅⨁π0​(A)​Z, a direct sum, never a product. The reason is that a singular chain is a finite linear combination of simplices. So for the compact space A={0}∪{1/n:n≥1}⊆RA=\{0\}\cup\{1/n:n\ge1\}\subseteq\RRA={0}∪{1/n:n≥1}⊆R, which has countably many components, H0(A)H_0(A)H0​(A) is countable — even though AAA is compact and looks like a limit of finite sets.

Example 5.4 (The shrinking wedge). Let H⊆R2\mathbb{H}\subseteq\RR^2H⊆R2 be the union of the circles CnC_nCn​ of radius 1/n1/n1/n centred at (1/n,0)(1/n,0)(1/n,0), all passing through the origin, with the subspace topology. Together the rnr_nrn​ give a homomorphism

Remark 5.6. Examples 5.3 and 5.4 are the two halves of a machine for breaking Theorem 5.1. Take a pair (X,A)(X,A)(X,A) with XXX contractible and AAA compact, totally disconnected and infinite. The hypothesis of Theorem 5.1 is therefore not removable, and the reader now has both computations needed to say so with a specific pair in hand.

5.2 Mayer–Vietoris

Theorem 5.7 (Mayer–Vietoris; recalled). If X=A˚∪B˚X=\mathring{A}\cup\mathring{B}X=A˚∪B˚ then there is a long exact sequence

⋯→Hn(A∩B)→  (i∗,−j∗)  Hn(A)⊕Hn(B)→  k∗+l∗  Hn(X)→ ∂ Hn−1(A∩B)→⋯\cdots\to H_n(A\cap B)\xrightarrow{\;(i_*,-j_*)\;}H_n(A)\oplus H_n(B) \xrightarrow{\;k_*+l_*\;}H_n(X)\xrightarrow{\ \partial\ }H_{n-1}(A\cap B)\to\cdots⋯→Hn​(A∩B)(i∗​,−j∗​)​Hn​(A)⊕Hn​(B)k∗​+l∗​​Hn​(X) ∂ ​Hn−1​(A∩B)→⋯

Remark 5.9. The arithmetic in Example 5.8 deserves naming, because it is what decides whether torsion appears. A cokernel Zk/⟨v⟩\ZZ^k/\langle v\rangleZk/⟨v⟩ is free exactly when vvv is primitive, i.e. when the gcd of its entries is 111; if v=d⋅v′v=d\cdot v'v=d⋅v′ with v′v'v′ primitive and d>1d>1d>1, the cokernel has a Z/d\ZZ/dZ/d summand. So when gluing along a circle, the question is never “is there a Möbius band?” but “what is the gcd of the entries of the gluing vector?”.

5.3 The Wang sequence

A mapping torus is glued from one piece along two copies of the fibre, and Mayer–Vietoris turns that into a single long exact sequence in which the monodromy appears as 1−f∗1-f_*1−f∗​.

Proposition 5.12 (Wang sequence). For the mapping torus TfT_fTf​ of f ⁣:X→Xf\colon X\to Xf:X→X there is a long exact sequence

⋯→Hn(X)→ 1−f∗ Hn(X)→Hn(Tf)→Hn−1(X)→ 1−f∗ Hn−1(X)→⋯\cdots\to H_n(X)\xrightarrow{\ 1-f_*\ }H_n(X)\to H_n(T_f)\to H_{n-1}(X) \xrightarrow{\ 1-f_*\ }H_{n-1}(X)\to\cdots⋯→Hn​(X) 1−f∗​ ​Hn​(X)→Hn​(Tf​)→Hn−1​(X) 1−f∗​ ​Hn−1​(X)→⋯

The Mayer–Vietoris map Hn(X)⊕Hn(X)→Hn(X)⊕Hn(X)H_n(X)\oplus H_n(X)\to H_n(X)\oplus H_n(X)Hn​(X)⊕Hn​(X)→Hn​(X)⊕Hn​(X) is (u,v)↦(u−v, u−f∗v)(u,v)\mapsto(u-v,\ u-f_*v)(u,v)↦(u−v, u−f∗​v) up to sign; changing basis by (u,v)↦(u−v,v)(u,v)\mapsto(u-v,v)(u,v)↦(u−v,v) turns it into a map whose cokernel and kernel are those of 1−f∗1-f_*1−f∗​, and the long exact sequence collapses to the one displayed. 5.4 What homology cannot see

Every computation above was insensitive to one thing: the way the pieces are attached in a way that is invisible on the level of chains. The sharpest form of this is that homology does not see the universal cover.

Example 5.16 (The universal cover of a wedge). Let X=S1∨S2X=S^1\vee S^2X=S1∨S2. Its universal cover X~\widetilde XX is the real line with a copy of S2S^2S2 attached at each integer: the line covers the circle, and each sheet of the covering carries its own copy of the sphere. Hence

Remark 5.18 (Where this chapter stops). H∗(X)H_*(X)H∗​(X) is a quotient of information about XXX that has already forgotten the covering. Two spaces can therefore agree in every homology group and disagree about their universal covers, hence about π2\pi_2π2​; and the two ingredients for producing such a pair are now both available — the universal cover of a wedge (Example 5.16) and the fact that a torus is aspherical. Constructing the pair, and proving the two spaces are not homotopy equivalent, is the natural exercise to attempt at this point.

The systematic repair is Chapter 13: replace H∗(X)H_*(X)H∗​(X) by H∗(X~)H_*(\widetilde X)H∗​(X) as a module over Z[π1X]\ZZ[\pi_1X]Z[π1​X], and the resulting invariant does detect homotopy equivalence for CW complexes.

5.5 What to carry forward

  • Hn(X,A)≅H~n(X/A)H_n(X,A)\cong\widetilde H_n(X/A)Hn​(X,A)≅Hn​(X/A) needs a neighbourhood deformation retract (Theorem 5.1); Examples 5.3 and 5.4 are the standard tools for showing it fails without one.

  • Mayer–Vietoris is arithmetic: torsion appears exactly when the gluing vector is imprimitive (Remark 5.9).

  • Heegaard gluings of two solid tori produce S1×S2S^1\times S^2S1×S2 and the lens spaces (Exercises 5.10 and 5.11) — the same spaces as Chapter 2, by a different route.

  • The Wang sequence (Proposition 5.12): coker⁡(1−f∗)\coker(1-f_*)coker(1−f∗​) below, ker⁡(1−f∗)\ker(1-f_*)ker(1−f∗​) above, and check the splitting before using it.

  • Homology does not see the universal cover (Remark 5.18); the module structure over Z[π1]\ZZ[\pi_1]Z[π1​] is what is missing.
