Chapter 10

H-spaces and Hopf algebras

In this chapter

  1. 10.1 The cohomology of an H-space
  2. 10.2 Primitives
  3. 10.3 Maps into an H-space, and power maps
  4. 10.4 What to carry forward

A multiplication on a space induces a comultiplication on its cohomology, and the resulting structure — a Hopf algebra — is rigid enough to answer questions that look purely geometric. This chapter is the first place in the book where an algebraic structure forbids something: Hopf’s theorem will show that most spheres carry no multiplication at all.

The other half of the chapter concerns maps rather than spaces. The multiplication on YYY makes [X,Y][X,Y][X,Y] into an algebraic object, and the kkk-th power map is then kkk times the identity in a precise sense. Where exactly that “kkk times” survives into cohomology is the delicate point, and Example 10.14 shows that the naive guess is false.

10.1 The cohomology of an H-space

Recall Definition 1.18: an H-space is a space XXX with basepoint eee and a map μ ⁣:X×X→X\mu\colon X\times X\to Xμ:X×X→X, with μ(e,e)=e\mu(e,e)=eμ(e,e)=e, for which μ(e,−)\mu(e,-)μ(e,−) and μ(−,e)\mu(-,e)μ(−,e) are homotopic to the identity.

Let kkk be a field and suppose H∗(X;k)H^*(X;k)H∗(X;k) is finite-dimensional in each degree, so that the Künneth theorem gives H∗(X×X;k)≅H∗(X;k)⊗H∗(X;k)H^*(X\times X;k)\cong H^*(X;k)\otimes H^*(X;k)H∗(X×X;k)≅H∗(X;k)⊗H∗(X;k). Then μ\muμ induces

μ∗ ⁣:H∗(X;k)⟶H∗(X;k)⊗H∗(X;k),\mu^*\colon H^*(X;k)\longrightarrow H^*(X;k)\otimes H^*(X;k),μ∗:H∗(X;k)⟶H∗(X;k)⊗H∗(X;k),

a coproduct. The unit conditions say that μ∗\mu^*μ∗ is counital, and H∗(X;k)H^*(X;k)H∗(X;k) becomes a graded Hopf algebra: a graded-commutative algebra with a coassociative coproduct that is a map of algebras.

Example 10.1 (Odd spheres). H∗(Sn;k)=Λ(x)H^*(S^n;k)=\Lambda(x)H∗(Sn;k)=Λ(x) with ∣x∣=n|x|=n∣x∣=n. There is nothing in degrees strictly between 000 and nnn, so the only possible terms in μ∗x\mu^*xμ∗x are x⊗1x\otimes1x⊗1 and 1⊗x1\otimes x1⊗x, and the counit conditions force both coefficients to be 111:

μ∗x=x⊗1+1⊗x.\mu^*x=x\otimes1+1\otimes x .μ∗x=x⊗1+1⊗x.

For nnn odd this is consistent: x2=0x^2=0x2=0 automatically by graded commutativity. For nnn even it is not, as Exercise 10.4 shows.

Example 10.2 (The torus and infinite projective space). T2T^2T2 is a topological group, and H∗(T2;k)=Λ(a,b)H^*(T^2;k)=\Lambda(a,b)H∗(T2;k)=Λ(a,b) with ∣a∣=∣b∣=1|a|=|b|=1∣a∣=∣b∣=1 and μ∗a=a⊗1+1⊗a\mu^*a=a\otimes1+1\otimes aμ∗a=a⊗1+1⊗a, similarly for bbb. Since μ∗\mu^*μ∗ is a ring map,

μ∗(ab)=(a⊗1+1⊗a)(b⊗1+1⊗b)=ab⊗1+a⊗b−b⊗a+1⊗ab,\mu^*(ab)=(a\otimes1+1\otimes a)(b\otimes1+1\otimes b) =ab\otimes1+a\otimes b-b\otimes a+1\otimes ab ,μ∗(ab)=(a⊗1+1⊗a)(b⊗1+1⊗b)=ab⊗1+a⊗b−b⊗a+1⊗ab,

the sign coming from moving bbb of degree one past aaa of degree one. So ababab has extra terms: not every class behaves like aaa and bbb do.

CP∞\CP^\inftyCP∞ is also an H-space — it is K(Z,2)K(\ZZ,2)K(Z,2), and the multiplication classifies the addition of degree-two cohomology classes — with H∗(CP∞;k)=k[c]H^*(\CP^\infty;k)=k[c]H∗(CP∞;k)=k[c], ∣c∣=2|c|=2∣c∣=2, and μ∗c=c⊗1+1⊗c\mu^*c=c\otimes1+1\otimes cμ∗c=c⊗1+1⊗c. Again μ∗(c2)=c2⊗1+2 c⊗c+1⊗c2\mu^*(c^2)=c^2\otimes1+2\,c\otimes c+1\otimes c^2μ∗(c2)=c2⊗1+2c⊗c+1⊗c2 has an extra term.

Theorem 10.3 (Hopf; recalled). Let AAA be a connected graded Hopf algebra over a field of characteristic zero which is finite-dimensional as a vector space. Then AAA is an exterior algebra Λ(x1,…,xr)\Lambda(x_1,\dots,x_r)Λ(x1​,…,xr​) on generators of odd degree.

Exercise 10.4 (medium). Deduce that SnS^nSn is not an H-space for any even n≥2n\ge2n≥2.

Hint. What would H∗(Sn;Q)H^*(S^n;\QQ)H∗(Sn;Q) have to be, as an algebra, if Theorem 10.3 applied?

Solution. Suppose SnS^nSn were an H-space with nnn even and n≥2n\ge2n≥2. Then A=H∗(Sn;Q)=Q{1,x}A=H^*(S^n;\QQ)=\QQ\{1,x\}A=H∗(Sn;Q)=Q{1,x} with ∣x∣=n|x|=n∣x∣=n even and x2=0x^2=0x2=0 would be a connected finite-dimensional graded Hopf algebra over Q\QQQ, so by Theorem 10.3 it would be an exterior algebra on odd generators. But AAA has a nonzero element in the even degree nnn and none in odd degrees, so its generators would have to include one of even degree nnn, which Theorem 10.3 forbids.

Equivalently and more concretely: for a free graded-commutative algebra a generator of even degree generates a polynomial algebra, which is infinite-dimensional, contradicting x2=0x^2=0x2=0.

Remark 10.5. S1S^1S1, S3S^3S3 and S7S^7S7 are H-spaces, being the unit spheres of the complex numbers, the quaternions and the octonions. Exercise 10.4 excludes the even spheres, and a much harder theorem — proved with the machinery of Chapter 16 and completed by Adams — excludes every odd sphere except those three.

10.2 Primitives

Definition 10.6. An element α∈H∗(X;k)\alpha\in H^*(X;k)α∈H∗(X;k) of positive degree is primitive if

μ∗α=α⊗1+1⊗α.\mu^*\alpha=\alpha\otimes1+1\otimes\alpha .μ∗α=α⊗1+1⊗α.

The primitives form a graded subspace P H∗(X;k)P\,H^*(X;k)PH∗(X;k).

Lemma 10.7 (Primitivity for degree reasons). If Hi(X;k)=0H^i(X;k)=0Hi(X;k)=0 for all 0<i<n0<i<n0<i<n, then every element of Hn(X;k)H^n(X;k)Hn(X;k) is primitive.

Proof. Write μ∗α=∑α′⊗α′′\mu^*\alpha=\sum\alpha'\otimes\alpha''μ∗α=∑α′⊗α′′ with the terms graded by (∣α′∣,∣α′′∣)(|\alpha'|,|\alpha''|)(∣α′∣,∣α′′∣) summing to nnn. The counit conditions supply the terms α⊗1\alpha\otimes1α⊗1 and 1⊗α1\otimes\alpha1⊗α; every other term has both factors of positive degree less than nnn, hence lies in a group that vanishes.□

Example 10.8. By Lemma 10.7, the generator xxx of Hn(Sn;k)H^n(S^n;k)Hn(Sn;k) is primitive. By the computations of Example 10.2, the primitives of Λ(a,b)=H∗(T2;k)\Lambda(a,b)=H^*(T^2;k)Λ(a,b)=H∗(T2;k) are exactly the span of aaa and bbb: the class ababab is not primitive, since μ∗(ab)\mu^*(ab)μ∗(ab) contains a⊗ba\otimes ba⊗b. Likewise the primitives of k[c]=H∗(CP∞;k)k[c]=H^*(\CP^\infty;k)k[c]=H∗(CP∞;k) in the case char k=0\mathrm{char}\,k=0chark=0 are exactly the span of ccc: for r≥2r\ge2r≥2,

μ∗(cr)=∑i=0r(ri)ci⊗cr−i,\mu^*(c^r)=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\binom{r}{i}c^{i}\otimes c^{r-i},μ∗(cr)=i=0∑r​(ir​)ci⊗cr−i,

which has nonzero middle terms.

Exercise 10.9 (easy). Let f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y be a map of H-spaces which commutes with the multiplications up to homotopy, i.e. f∘μX≃μY∘(f×f)f\circ\mu_X\simeq\mu_Y\circ(f\times f)f∘μX​≃μY​∘(f×f). Show that f∗f^*f∗ carries primitives to primitives.

Solution. Let α∈P H∗(Y;k)\alpha\in P\,H^*(Y;k)α∈PH∗(Y;k). Then

μX∗(f∗α)=(f∘μX)∗α=(μY∘(f×f))∗α=(f∗⊗f∗)μY∗α,\mu_X^*\big(f^*\alpha\big)=\big(f\circ\mu_X\big)^*\alpha =\big(\mu_Y\circ(f\times f)\big)^*\alpha =(f^*\otimes f^*)\mu_Y^*\alpha ,μX∗​(f∗α)=(f∘μX​)∗α=(μY​∘(f×f))∗α=(f∗⊗f∗)μY∗​α,

10.3 Maps into an H-space, and power maps

Definition 10.10. For maps f,g ⁣:X→Yf,g\colon X\to Yf,g:X→Y into an H-space YYY, their pointwise product is

fg=μ∘(f,g) ⁣:X⟶Y,(fg)(x)=μ(f(x),g(x)).fg=\mu\circ(f,g)\colon X\longrightarrow Y,\qquad (fg)(x)=\mu\big(f(x),g(x)\big).fg=μ∘(f,g):X⟶Y,(fg)(x)=μ(f(x),g(x)).

The kkk-th power map of YYY is pk ⁣:Y→Yp_k\colon Y\to Ypk​:Y→Y, the kkk-fold pointwise product of the identity with itself.

The pointwise product descends to homotopy classes, so [X,Y][X,Y][X,Y] carries a binary operation with a two-sided unit. If the multiplication is homotopy-associative and admits a homotopy inverse — as it does for a topological group — then [X,Y][X,Y][X,Y] is a group, naturally in XXX.

Remark 10.11. The case to keep in mind is Y=K(G,n)Y=K(G,n)Y=K(G,n). There [X,K(G,n)]≅Hn(X;G)[X,K(G,n)]\cong H^n(X;G)[X,K(G,n)]≅Hn(X;G), a fact proved in Chapter 18, and under that identification the pointwise product is the addition of cohomology classes. The group structure on cohomology and the H-space structure on an Eilenberg–MacLane space are the same thing seen from two sides.

Example 10.12 (The baby case). For Y=S1Y=S^1Y=S1 with its group structure, pkp_kpk​ is z↦zkz\mapsto z^kz↦zk. On π1(S1)=Z\pi_1(S^1)=\ZZπ1​(S1)=Z it is multiplication by kkk: the loop t↦e2πitt\mapsto e^{2\pi it}t↦e2πit is sent to t↦e2πiktt\mapsto e^{2\pi ikt}t↦e2πikt, which winds kkk times. Hence deg⁡pk=k\deg p_k=kdegpk​=k.

That computation generalises, and the general statement is about homotopy groups.

Exercise 10.13 (medium). Let GGG be a topological group (or any homotopy-associative H-space with a homotopy inverse). Show that for every n≥1n\ge1n≥1 the kkk-th power map induces multiplication by kkk on πn(G)\pi_n(G)πn​(G).

Hint. πn(G)\pi_n(G)πn​(G) carries two products, concatenation and the pointwise one, and by the Eckmann–Hilton argument of Chapter 1 they coincide. Which one is pk∘fp_k\circ fpk​∘f obviously built from?

Solution. Let α∈πn(G)\alpha\in\pi_n(G)α∈πn​(G) be represented by f ⁣:Sn→Gf\colon S^n\to Gf:Sn→G. By definition of the power map, pk∘fp_k\circ fpk​∘f is the kkk-fold pointwise product f⋅f⋯ff\cdot f\cdots ff⋅f⋯f, so [pk∘f][p_k\circ f][pk​∘f] is the kkk-fold product of [f][f][f] under the pointwise operation on πn(G)\pi_n(G)πn​(G). By Proposition 1.19 — whose proof applies verbatim to πn\pi_nπn​ — the pointwise operation agrees with concatenation, which is the group law of πn(G)\pi_n(G)πn​(G). Hence pk∗(α)=kαp_{k*}(\alpha)=k\alphapk∗​(α)=kα.

Now the point of the chapter. The corresponding statement in cohomology is false in general, and it fails in the smallest example.

Example 10.14 (The power map on a torus). Take G=T2G=T^2G=T2 and H∗(T2;Z)=Λ(a,b)H^*(T^2;\ZZ)=\Lambda(a,b)H∗(T2;Z)=Λ(a,b) as in Example 10.2. The power map pkp_kpk​ is (z,w)↦(zk,wk)(z,w)\mapsto(z^k,w^k)(z,w)↦(zk,wk), so by Example 10.12 applied in each factor,

pk∗a=ka,pk∗b=kb,pk∗(ab)=(ka)(kb)=k2 ab.p_k^*a=ka,\qquad p_k^*b=kb,\qquad p_k^*(ab)=(ka)(kb)=k^2\,ab .pk∗​a=ka,pk∗​b=kb,pk∗​(ab)=(ka)(kb)=k2ab.

So pkp_kpk​ acts by kkk in degree one and by k2k^2k2 in degree two; its degree as a self-map of the surface T2T^2T2 is k2k^2k2, not kkk.

Compare Example 10.8: the classes on which pk∗p_k^*pk∗​ is multiplication by kkk are exactly aaa and bbb, the primitives, and ababab — the one class that failed to be primitive — is exactly the one where the factor is k2k^2k2 instead.

Remark 10.15 (The pattern, and what to do with it). Example 10.14 is not an accident of the torus. The pointwise product of two maps is μ∘(f,g)\mu\circ(f,g)μ∘(f,g), so computing (fg)∗(fg)^*(fg)∗ means evaluating μ∗\mu^*μ∗ and then f∗⊗g∗f^*\otimes g^*f∗⊗g∗; and μ∗α\mu^*\alphaμ∗α has exactly two terms precisely when α\alphaα is primitive. On a primitive class the two contributions add; on a non-primitive class the cross terms survive and produce something else — k2k^2k2 in the example above.

Turning that observation into a proposition, and then reading off what the kkk-th power map does to the primitives of a specific H-space, is the natural continuation of this chapter. Lemma 10.7 identifies the primitives of a sphere in one line, and Example 10.12 shows what the answer must specialise to. Note that Exercise 10.13 gives no information here beyond the top degree, since the higher homotopy of an H-space and its cohomology are quite different objects; the cohomological statement has to be proved cohomologically.

Remark 10.16 (Forward). The Hopf algebra structure of this chapter is used three more times. In Chapter 26 the H-space structure of CP∞=K(Z,2)\CP^\infty=K(\ZZ,2)CP∞=K(Z,2) is what makes the Euler class of a tensor product of line bundles additive. In Chapter 16 the failure of most spheres to be H-spaces is refined into the Hopf invariant one problem. And in Chapter 31 the same structure reappears rationally, where Hopf’s theorem becomes the statement that the rational homotopy of an H-space is its space of primitives.

10.4 What to carry forward

  • A multiplication on XXX makes H∗(X;k)H^*(X;k)H∗(X;k) a Hopf algebra (Examples 10.1 and 10.2); Hopf’s theorem then constrains which spaces admit one (Theorem 10.3 and Exercise 10.4).

  • Primitives (Definition 10.6); low degrees are primitive for free (Lemma 10.7); H-maps preserve them (Exercise 10.9).

  • The kkk-th power map is multiplication by kkk on homotopy groups (Exercise 10.13) but not on cohomology (Example 10.14); primitivity is exactly the condition that repairs the discrepancy (Remark 10.15).
