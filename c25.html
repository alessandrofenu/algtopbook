Chapter 25

Operads and homotopy Lie algebras

In this chapter

  1. 25.1 Operads
  2. 25.2 The homotopy Lie algebra
  3. 25.3 The dichotomy
  4. 25.4 What to carry forward

An operad records the operations of an algebraic structure together with the ways they can be composed. The formalism costs something to set up and repays it twice over: it makes “algebra up to homotopy” definable, and it makes the rational homotopy of a space into an algebraic object with its own structure theory.

This chapter does the minimum of the first and the beginning of the second. The target is the homotopy Lie algebra of a space — π∗(ΩX)⊗Q\pi_*(\Omega X)\otimes\QQπ∗​(ΩX)⊗Q with the Whitehead product — and the dichotomy it produces: a simply connected finite complex is either elliptic, with finite-dimensional rational homotopy, or hyperbolic, with rational homotopy growing exponentially. There is nothing in between.

25.1 Operads

Definition 25.1. An operad P\mathcal{P}P in vector spaces consists of vector spaces P(n)\mathcal{P}(n)P(n) with an action of the symmetric group Sn\mathbb{S}_nSn​, a unit id∈P(1)\mathrm{id}\in\mathcal{P}(1)id∈P(1), and composition maps

∘i ⁣:P(m)⊗P(n)⟶P(m+n−1),1≤i≤m,\circ_i\colon\mathcal{P}(m)\otimes\mathcal{P}(n)\longrightarrow \mathcal{P}(m+n-1),\qquad 1\le i\le m,∘i​:P(m)⊗P(n)⟶P(m+n−1),1≤i≤m,

— plug the second operation into the iii-th input of the first — satisfying associativity, unitality and equivariance axioms.

Think of P(n)\mathcal{P}(n)P(n) as the space of nnn-ary operations of some kind of algebra, with Sn\mathbb{S}_nSn​ permuting the inputs.

Example 25.2 (The two basic operads). Ass⁡(n)=k[Sn]\Ass(n)=k[\mathbb{S}_n]Ass(n)=k[Sn​], the regular representation, with ∘i\circ_i∘i​ given by substitution of words: an algebra over Ass⁡\AssAss is an associative algebra, and the n!n!n! basis elements record the n!n!n! ways of ordering the inputs of an nnn-fold product.

Com⁡(n)=k\Com(n)=kCom(n)=k, the trivial representation: an algebra over Com⁡\ComCom is a commutative associative algebra, and there is only one nnn-ary operation because the order of the inputs does not matter.

Example 25.3 (Comparing them). There is a morphism of operads Ass⁡→Com⁡\Ass\to\ComAss→Com: in each arity it is the map k[Sn]→kk[\mathbb{S}_n]\to kk[Sn​]→k sending every permutation to 111. It is compatible with ∘i\circ_i∘i​, because substituting words and then forgetting the order gives the same result as forgetting the order throughout.

That is the direction that works, and it corresponds to the evident fact that a commutative algebra is in particular an associative one.

Warning 25.4 (Arity-wise maps are not operad morphisms). The opposite direction is tempting and wrong. In each arity there is an evident equivariant map Com⁡(n)→Ass⁡(n)\Com(n)\to\Ass(n)Com(n)→Ass(n), embedding the trivial representation in the regular one by sending the generator to the sum of all permutations. It is a map of S\mathbb{S}S-modules, so it looks like a candidate.

It is not an operad morphism, and the reason is a counting mismatch: composing two such sums produces fewer terms than the sum attached to the composite arity, because the sum over Sm\mathbb{S}_mSm​ composed with the sum over Sn\mathbb{S}_nSn​ does not exhaust Sm+n−1\mathbb{S}_{m+n-1}Sm+n−1​. Checking this on the smallest interesting case — two binary operations composed into a ternary one — is a short computation and a good corrective: it shows that being a morphism of S\mathbb{S}S-modules in every arity is strictly weaker than respecting ∘i\circ_i∘i​.

25.2 The homotopy Lie algebra

Definition 25.5. For a simply connected space XXX, the graded vector space

LX=π∗(ΩX)⊗QL_X=\pi_*(\Omega X)\otimes\QQLX​=π∗​(ΩX)⊗Q

carries a graded Lie bracket, induced by the Whitehead product of Definition 19.6 under the isomorphism πk(ΩX)≅πk+1(X)\pi_k(\Omega X)\cong\pi_{k+1}(X)πk​(ΩX)≅πk+1​(X). It is the homotopy Lie algebra of XXX.

Example 25.6. For X=SnX=S^nX=Sn with nnn odd, LXL_XLX​ is one-dimensional, spanned by a class in degree n−1n-1n−1, with zero bracket. For nnn even it is two-dimensional, spanned by classes in degrees n−1n-1n−1 and 2n−22n-22n−2, and the bracket of the first with itself is the second — this is the Whitehead square of Remark 19.9, appearing now as a Lie bracket. Example 24.5 recorded the same two cases as one generator versus two.

Theorem 25.7 (Milnor–Moore; recalled). For XXX simply connected of finite Q\QQQ-type there is an isomorphism of graded Hopf algebras

U(LX) ≅ H∗(ΩX;Q),U\big(L_X\big)\ \cong\ H_*\big(\Omega X;\QQ\big),U(LX​) ≅ H∗​(ΩX;Q),

where UUU is the universal enveloping algebra.

So the homology of a loop space is determined by the homotopy Lie algebra, and conversely — LXL_XLX​ is the space of primitives, in the sense of Definition 10.6. Counting one therefore counts the other, and that is the mechanism behind the dichotomy.

Proposition 25.8 (Free Lie algebras). For a graded vector space VVV there is a free graded Lie algebra L(V)\mathbb{L}(V)L(V), and

UL(V) ≅ T(V),U\mathbb{L}(V)\ \cong\ T(V),UL(V) ≅ T(V),

the free associative algebra on VVV.

Exercise 25.9 (medium). Prove Proposition 25.8, and explain why the proof says nothing about the size of L(V)\mathbb{L}(V)L(V) itself.

Hint. UUU is left adjoint to the functor sending an associative algebra to its commutator Lie algebra, and L\mathbb{L}L is left adjoint to the forgetful functor to graded vector spaces.

Solution. Both ULU\mathbb{L}UL and TTT are left adjoints of the same functor: for an associative algebra AAA,

Alg(UL(V),A)≅Lie(L(V),ALie)≅Vect(V,A)≅Alg(T(V),A),\mathrm{Alg}\big(U\mathbb{L}(V),A\big)\cong\mathrm{Lie}\big(\mathbb{L}(V),A_{\mathrm{Lie}}\big) \cong\mathrm{Vect}\big(V,A\big)\cong\mathrm{Alg}\big(T(V),A\big),Alg(UL(V),A)≅Lie(L(V),ALie​)≅Vect(V,A)≅Alg(T(V),A),

naturally in AAA; a left adjoint is unique up to isomorphism, so UL(V)≅T(V)U\mathbb{L}(V)\cong T(V)UL(V)≅T(V).

The computation determines the enveloping algebra completely and says nothing directly about L(V)\mathbb{L}(V)L(V), which sits inside T(V)T(V)T(V) as the sub-Lie-algebra generated by VVV — a much smaller and less obvious object.

Theorem 25.10 (Graded Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt; recalled). Work over a field of characteristic zero, and let LLL be a graded Lie algebra. Then U(L)U(L)U(L) agrees, as a graded vector space, with the free graded commutative algebra generated by LLL: a polynomial algebra on the generators of even degree, tensored with an exterior algebra on those of odd degree. In terms of Poincaré series, writing Lk=dim⁡LkL_k=\dim L_kLk​=dimLk​,

∑ndim⁡U(L)n tn=∏k even(1−tk)−Lk ⋅∏k odd(1+tk)Lk.\sum_n\dim U(L)_n\,t^n =\prod_{k\ \mathrm{even}}\big(1-t^{k}\big)^{-L_k}\ \cdot \prod_{k\ \mathrm{odd}}\big(1+t^{k}\big)^{L_k}.n∑​dimU(L)n​tn=k even∏​(1−tk)−Lk​ ⋅k odd∏​(1+tk)Lk​.

Warning 25.11 (The parity is not optional). The two products in Theorem 25.10 differ, and using the ungraded form of the theorem — polynomial on everything — gives wrong dimensions in every odd degree. This is the same parity that separated the two cases of Example 24.5 and produced the extra generator of Warning 19.5; by now it should be automatic.

Remark 25.12 (Counting a free Lie algebra). Combining Proposition 25.8 and Theorem 25.10 gives a way to compute the dimensions of a free graded Lie algebra. The Poincaré series of T(V)T(V)T(V) is elementary: ∑n(dim⁡V)ntn\sum_n(\dim V)^nt^{n}∑n​(dimV)ntn suitably graded, a geometric series. The Poincaré series of UL(V)U\mathbb{L}(V)UL(V) is that same series, by Proposition 25.8. And Theorem 25.10 expresses it as a product over the unknown dimensions dim⁡L(V)k\dim\mathbb{L}(V)_kdimL(V)k​. Equating the two and inverting determines those dimensions one degree at a time.

Carrying this out for VVV of dimension two — the case X=Sn∨SmX=S^n\vee S^mX=Sn∨Sm, where the homotopy Lie algebra is free on two generators — is the natural exercise. Two things are worth noticing while doing it. The geometric series has radius of convergence less than one as soon as dim⁡V≥2\dim V\ge2dimV≥2, which is what forces the answer to grow; and a lower bound suffices for the qualitative conclusion, so there is no need for a closed formula.

25.3 The dichotomy

Definition 25.13. A simply connected finite CW complex XXX is elliptic if dim⁡Qπ∗(X)⊗Q<∞\dim_\QQ\pi_*(X)\otimes\QQ<\inftydimQ​π∗​(X)⊗Q<∞, and hyperbolic otherwise.

Example 25.14. Spheres are elliptic, by Example 24.18: their rational homotopy has dimension one or two. So are CPn\CP^nCPn, Lie groups, and homogeneous spaces — all of them have minimal models with finitely many generators, and Theorem 24.16 then bounds the rational homotopy.

Theorem 25.15 (Dichotomy; recalled). A simply connected finite complex is either elliptic, or hyperbolic with

∑k≤rdim⁡Qπk(X)⊗Q\sum_{k\le r}\dim_\QQ\pi_k(X)\otimes\QQk≤r∑​dimQ​πk​(X)⊗Q

growing exponentially in rrr. No intermediate growth occurs.

Remark 25.16 (Where the simplest hyperbolic example is). A wedge of two spheres has free homotopy Lie algebra — freeness is what a wedge does, exactly as π1\pi_1π1​ of a wedge of circles is free — so Remark 25.12 applies to it directly. Working out the growth for Sn∨SmS^n\vee S^mSn∨Sm shows that the simplest finite complex beyond a single sphere is already hyperbolic, and that its rational homotopy is enormous: a two-cell complex with an infinite-dimensional rational homotopy, growing exponentially.

That is worth pausing on. Chapter 14 could compute π3(S2)\pi_3(S^2)π3​(S2) and stopped; here one gets the full rational homotopy of a whole family of spaces, at the cost of tensoring with Q\QQQ and of the machinery of Parts III and IV. The trade is characteristic of the subject.

Remark 25.17 (Forward). Three threads continue into Part V. The composition product of Definition 25.1 is made explicit in Chapter 30, where it becomes a computation with induced representations. The notion of an algebra “up to homotopy” over an operad is constructed there too, by a duality that turns P\mathcal{P}P into a cooperad. And Chapter 31 returns to Theorem 25.7 and to UUU, where the four standard properties of the universal enveloping algebra are derived from the single adjunction used in Exercise 25.9.

25.4 What to carry forward

  • Operads (Definition 25.1); Ass⁡\AssAss and Com⁡\ComCom (Example 25.2), with Ass⁡→Com⁡\Ass\to\ComAss→Com the direction that works (Example 25.3 and Warning 25.4).

  • The homotopy Lie algebra (Definition 25.5), and Milnor–Moore identifying its enveloping algebra with H∗(ΩX;Q)H_*(\Omega X;\QQ)H∗​(ΩX;Q) (Theorem 25.7).

  • UL(V)=T(V)U\mathbb{L}(V)=T(V)UL(V)=T(V) from adjunctions (Exercise 25.9); graded PBW converts that into dimensions (Theorem 25.10, Warning 25.11, and Remark 25.12).

  • The elliptic/hyperbolic dichotomy (Definition 25.13 and Theorem 25.15); wedges are the simplest hyperbolic examples (Remark 25.16).
