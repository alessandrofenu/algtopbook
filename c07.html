Chapter 7

Products and duality

In this chapter

  2. 7.2 Poincaré duality
  3. 7.3 Where the ring fails

Three invariants have now failed on the same pair of spaces. In Chapter 2 the fundamental group did not distinguish L(5,1)L(5,1)L(5,1) from L(5,2)L(5,2)L(5,2); in Chapter 4 their homology turned out not to involve qqq at all. The reason, in both cases, was structural: those invariants are assembled from the cells one at a time, and lens spaces differ in how their cells are linked.

This chapter introduces the first invariant with a multiplication. It succeeds where the groups fail — distinguishing spaces with identical cohomology groups — and it also fails, on the same lens spaces, in a way that will take until Chapter 18 to repair. Both halves are the point.

7.1 The cup product

We take the construction as known: H∗(X;R)=⨁nHn(X;R)H^*(X;R)=\bigoplus_nH^n(X;R)H∗(X;R)=⨁n​Hn(X;R) carries an associative product, natural in XXX, with unit 1∈H01\in H^01∈H0, satisfying

α⌣β=(−1)∣α∣∣β∣ β⌣α.\alpha\smile\beta=(-1)^{|\alpha||\beta|}\,\beta\smile\alpha .α⌣β=(−1)∣α∣∣β∣β⌣α.

Naturality means f∗(α⌣β)=f∗α⌣f∗βf^*(\alpha\smile\beta)=f^*\alpha\smile f^*\betaf∗(α⌣β)=f∗α⌣f∗β: a map of spaces induces a map of rings, and that is where all the power comes from.

Example 7.1 (The torus). H∗(T2;Z)H^*(T^2;\ZZ)H∗(T2;Z) is the exterior algebra Λ(a,b)\Lambda(a,b)Λ(a,b) on two generators of degree one: a2=b2=0a^2=b^2=0a2=b2=0 by graded commutativity, ab=−baab=-baab=−ba, and ababab generates H2(T2)≅ZH^2(T^2)\cong\ZZH2(T2)≅Z. The last assertion — that the product of the two one-dimensional generators is a generator, not merely some element — is the content, and it is what Theorem 7.5 will explain.

Example 7.2 (Complex projective plane against a wedge). H∗(CP2;Z)=Z[x]/(x3)H^*(\CP^2;\ZZ)=\ZZ[x]/(x^3)H∗(CP2;Z)=Z[x]/(x3) with ∣x∣=2|x|=2∣x∣=2, so the groups are Z,0,Z,0,Z\ZZ,0,\ZZ,0,\ZZZ,0,Z,0,Z in degrees 0,…,40,\dots,40,…,4 and x2x^2x2 generates H4H^4H4.

The wedge S2∨S4S^2\vee S^4S2∨S4 has exactly the same cohomology groups. But its ring is different: writing yyy for the generator of H2(S2∨S4)H^2(S^2\vee S^4)H2(S2∨S4), we have y2=0y^2=0y2=0. Indeed yyy restricts to a generator on S2S^2S2 and to 000 on S4S^4S4, so y2y^2y2 restricts to 000 on both — on S2S^2S2 because H4(S2)=0H^4(S^2)=0H4(S2)=0 — and restriction to the two summands is injective on H~∗(S2∨S4)\widetilde H^*(S^2\vee S^4)H∗(S2∨S4).

So CP2≄S2∨S4\CP^2\not\simeq S^2\vee S^4CP2≃S2∨S4: the groups cannot see the difference and the ring can.

The mechanism in that example is worth isolating, because it is the only thing one ever uses about wedges.

Exercise 7.3 (easy). Show that H~∗(X∨Y;R)≅H~∗(X;R)⊕H~∗(Y;R)\widetilde H^*(X\vee Y;R)\cong\widetilde H^*(X;R)\oplus\widetilde H^*(Y;R)H∗(X∨Y;R)≅H∗(X;R)⊕H∗(Y;R) as graded groups, and that under this isomorphism the product of a class coming from XXX with a class coming from YYY is zero.

Exercise 7.4 (medium). Show that S2×S2S^2\times S^2S2×S2 and S2∨S2∨S4S^2\vee S^2\vee S^4S2∨S2∨S4 have isomorphic cohomology groups in every degree but non-isomorphic cohomology rings, and deduce that they are not homotopy equivalent.

Hint. By Künneth, H∗(S2×S2)H^*(S^2\times S^2)H∗(S2×S2) has generators a,ba,ba,b in degree 222 with a2=b2=0a^2=b^2=0a2=b2=0. What is ababab?

Solution. For the product, Künneth gives H∗(S2×S2;Z)=Z[a,b]/(a2,b2)H^*(S^2\times S^2;\ZZ)=\ZZ[a,b]/(a^2,b^2)H∗(S2×S2;Z)=Z[a,b]/(a2,b2) with ∣a∣=∣b∣=2|a|=|b|=2∣a∣=∣b∣=2, so the groups are Z,0,Z2,0,Z\ZZ,0,\ZZ^2,0,\ZZZ,0,Z2,0,Z and ababab generates H4H^4H4. A ring isomorphism would have to carry a pair of degree-222 generators to a pair of degree-222 generators and preserve their product, which is a generator of H4H^4H4 on one side and 000 on the other. So the rings differ, and since H∗(−;Z)H^*(-;\ZZ)H∗(−;Z) is a homotopy-invariant functor to rings, the spaces are not homotopy equivalent.

7.2 Poincaré duality

Theorem 7.5 (Poincaré duality; recalled). Let MMM be a closed connected nnn-manifold.

  1. If MMM is orientable, capping with the fundamental class gives an isomorphism

    Hk(M;Z)  ≅  Hn−k(M;Z)for all k.H^k(M;\ZZ)\;\cong\;H_{n-k}(M;\ZZ)\qquad\text{for all }k.Hk(M;Z)≅Hn−k​(M;Z)for all k.

  2. With no orientability hypothesis at all, the same holds over F2\FF_2F2​:

    Hk(M;F2)  ≅  Hn−k(M;F2).H^k(M;\FF_2)\;\cong\;H_{n-k}(M;\FF_2).Hk(M;F2​)≅Hn−k​(M;F2​).

In the orientable case the pairing Hk(M;Z)⊗Hn−k(M;Z)→Hn(M;Z)≅ZH^k(M;\ZZ)\otimes H^{n-k}(M;\ZZ)\to H^n(M;\ZZ)\cong\ZZHk(M;Z)⊗Hn−k(M;Z)→Hn(M;Z)≅Z given by the cup product is nondegenerate modulo torsion; with F2\FF_2F2​ coefficients it is nondegenerate.

The nondegeneracy statement is what makes the cup product computable on manifolds: it says a nonzero class in HkH^kHk must have a partner it pairs nontrivially with. Example 7.1 is the smallest instance.

Exercise 7.6 (easy). Show that H1(X;Z)H^1(X;\ZZ)H1(X;Z) is torsion-free for every space XXX, and free abelian if H1(X)H_1(X)H1​(X) is finitely generated.

Solution. By Theorem 6.1, H1(X;Z)H^1(X;\ZZ)H1(X;Z) is an extension of Hom⁡(H1X,Z)\Hom(H_1X,\ZZ)Hom(H1​X,Z) by Ext⁡(H0X,Z)\Ext(H_0X,\ZZ)Ext(H0​X,Z), and H0XH_0XH0​X is free abelian, so Ext⁡(H0X,Z)=0\Ext(H_0X,\ZZ)=0Ext(H0​X,Z)=0 and H1(X;Z)≅Hom⁡(H1X,Z)H^1(X;\ZZ)\cong\Hom(H_1X,\ZZ)H1(X;Z)≅Hom(H1​X,Z). Corollary 7.7. For a closed orientable nnn-manifold MMM, the group Hn−1(M;Z)H_{n-1}(M;\ZZ)Hn−1​(M;Z) is free abelian.

Proof. Hn−1(M;Z)≅H1(M;Z)H_{n-1}(M;\ZZ)\cong H^1(M;\ZZ)Hn−1​(M;Z)≅H1(M;Z) by Theorem 7.5(a), which is free by Exercise 7.6, the homology of a closed manifold being finitely generated.□

Exercise 7.8 (medium). Show that χ(M)=0\chi(M)=0χ(M)=0 for every closed manifold of odd dimension, orientable or not.

Compute χ(M)=∑k(−1)kdim⁡F2Hk(M;F2)\chi(M)=\sum_k(-1)^k\dim_{\FF_2}H_k(M;\FF_2)χ(M)=∑k​(−1)kdimF2​​Hk​(M;F2​), which agrees with the integral Euler characteristic because both equal the alternating sum of the numbers of cells. Assembling this is the natural way to finish the chapter’s duality section, and every ingredient is now on the page.

7.3 Where the ring fails

Example 7.10 (Lens spaces, again). By Example 4.8 and Theorem 6.1,

H∗(L(p,q);Z)=(Z, 0, Z/p, Z)H^*(L(p,q);\ZZ)=\big(\ZZ,\ 0,\ \ZZ/p,\ \ZZ\big)H∗(L(p,q);Z)=(Z, 0, Z/p, Z)

in degrees 0,1,2,30,1,2,30,1,2,3. The multiplication is forced and carries no information: the only products of positive-degree classes to compute land in H4=0H^4=0H4=0 or pair H2⊗H2→H4=0H^2\otimes H^2\to H^4=0H2⊗H2→H4=0. So the integral cohomology ring of L(p,q)L(p,q)L(p,q), like its homology and its fundamental group, is independent of qqq.

Yet L(5,1)L(5,1)L(5,1) and L(5,2)L(5,2)L(5,2) are not homotopy equivalent. This is a classical theorem: L(p,q)L(p,q)L(p,q) and L(p,q′)L(p,q')L(p,q′) are homotopy equivalent if and only if qq′≡±n2(modp)qq'\equiv\pm n^2\pmod pqq′≡±n2(modp) for some integer nnn, and for p=5p=5p=5 the squares mod 555 are {0,1,4}\{0,1,4\}{0,1,4}, which is closed under negation, while qq′=2qq'=2qq′=2.

Warning 7.11. The book does not prove the classification just quoted, and the reader should not pretend otherwise. What separates these spaces is a refinement of the cup product called the linking form, a Q/Z\QQ/\ZZQ/Z-valued pairing on the torsion of H1H_1H1​, which is invisible to the integral ring because that ring is trivial in the relevant degrees. What is worth extracting here is only the shape of the failure: a product can be forced to vanish for degree reasons and therefore carry no information, while a finer operation on the same cohomology does.

Remark 7.12 (The question Part I ends on). Example 7.2 and Exercise 7.4 showed that the cohomology ring is strictly stronger than the cohomology groups. Example 7.10 shows it is not complete. There are even pairs of spaces with isomorphic cohomology rings, over every coefficient ring at once, that are not homotopy equivalent; producing one is not hard once one knows where to look, and recognising that the ring has run out is the first step.

What replaces it is an operation that is natural but not induced by any multiplication: a family of maps Hn(X;F2)→Hn+i(X;F2)H^n(X;\FF_2)\to H^{n+i}(X;\FF_2)Hn(X;F2​)→Hn+i(X;F2​) commuting with f∗f^*f∗ for every fff. These are the Steenrod squares, and Chapter 18 constructs them and uses them to settle a case that the ring provably cannot. Part II builds the machinery they need.

7.4 What to carry forward

  • The cup product makes f∗f^*f∗ a ring map; that naturality is the whole source of its strength.

  • On a wedge, classes from different summands multiply to zero (Exercise 7.3) — the standard obstruction.

  • CP2\CP^2CP2 against S2∨S4S^2\vee S^4S2∨S4, and S2×S2S^2\times S^2S2×S2 against S2∨S2∨S4S^2\vee S^2\vee S^4S2∨S2∨S4: same groups, different rings (Example 7.2 and Exercise 7.4).

  • Duality (Theorem 7.5): orientable over Z\ZZZ, always over F2\FF_2F2​; the cup pairing is nondegenerate, so nonzero classes have partners.

  • H1H^1H1 is torsion-free (Exercise 7.6), Hn−1H_{n-1}Hn−1​ of a closed orientable manifold is free (Corollary 7.7), and χ=0\chi=0χ=0 in odd dimensions (Exercise 7.8).

  • The ring is not a complete invariant either (Example 7.10 and Remark 7.12); the repair is Chapter 18.
