Chapter 6

Cohomology, coefficients, and the failure of naturality

In this chapter

  1. 6.1 The universal coefficient theorem
  2. 6.2 Moore spaces
  3. 6.3 A natural sequence with an unnatural splitting
  4. 6.4 What to carry forward

Cohomology is not merely homology with the arrows reversed. The universal coefficient theorem says how much of H∗H^*H∗ is determined by H∗H_*H∗​ — almost all of it — and the residue, an Ext⁡\ExtExt term sitting one degree up, is exactly what makes cohomology a strictly finer invariant of maps than homology.

The chapter is built around one distinction, and it is worth stating before any mathematics: a short exact sequence can be natural, and can split, without the splitting being natural. Readers meet this first as a piece of pedantry and later as the reason a whole subject exists. It recurs in Chapter 8 for the Künneth sequence, and the invariant that non-naturality has been concealing is finally constructed in Chapter 18.

6.1 The universal coefficient theorem

Theorem 6.1 (Universal coefficients; recalled). For a chain complex of free abelian groups and any abelian group GGG there is a short exact sequence, natural in the complex,

0⟶Ext⁡(Hn−1(X),G)⟶Hn(X;G)→ h Hom⁡(Hn(X),G)⟶0,0\longrightarrow\Ext\big(H_{n-1}(X),G\big)\longrightarrow H^n(X;G) \xrightarrow{\ h\ }\Hom\big(H_n(X),G\big)\longrightarrow0 ,0⟶Ext(Hn−1​(X),G)⟶Hn(X;G) h ​Hom(Hn​(X),G)⟶0,

The practical content is that HnH^nHn is Hom⁡\HomHom of HnH_nHn​ plus Ext⁡\ExtExt of Hn−1H_{n-1}Hn−1​: free parts stay where they are, torsion moves up one degree.

6.2 Moore spaces

To probe a theorem about H∗H_*H∗​ one wants spaces with prescribed homology, and they exist.

Proposition 6.4. For every abelian group AAA and every n≥1n\ge1n≥1 there is a CW complex M(A,n)M(A,n)M(A,n), with cells only in dimensions nnn and n+1n+1n+1 besides a single 000-cell, such that

H~i(M(A,n))={A,i=n,0,i≠n.\widetilde H_i\big(M(A,n)\big)=\begin{cases}A,&i=n,\\0,&i\neq n.\end{cases}Hi​(M(A,n))={A,0,​i=n,i=n.​

Remark 6.5. Two features will be used repeatedly. First, M(A,n)M(A,n)M(A,n) exists for arbitrary AAA — there is no finite generation hypothesis, because Z\ZZZ is a principal ideal domain and resolutions have length one. Second, Hn+1(M(A,n))=0H_{n+1}(M(A,n))=0Hn+1​(M(A,n))=0 by construction, which is what makes these spaces useful as test objects: they have homology in exactly one degree, so any invariant that is nonzero elsewhere is detecting something other than homology. Chapter 8 uses both facts to prove a theorem of pure algebra by topological means.

6.3 A natural sequence with an unnatural splitting

What exactly is being claimed when one says the splitting is not natural? The following turns the claim into something testable.

Proposition 6.7. Suppose the splitting of Theorem 6.1 could be chosen naturally, i.e. so that for every map f ⁣:X→Yf\colon X\to Yf:X→Y the induced maps commute with the chosen isomorphisms Hn(−;Z)≅Hom⁡(Hn,Z)⊕Ext⁡(Hn−1,Z)H^n(-;\ZZ)\cong\Hom(H_n,\ZZ)\oplus\Ext(H_{n-1},\ZZ)Hn(−;Z)≅Hom(Hn​,Z)⊕Ext(Hn−1​,Z). Then any map fff with H~∗(f)=0\widetilde H_*(f)=0H∗​(f)=0 would satisfy H~∗(f)=0\widetilde H^*(f)=0H∗(f)=0.

Proof. A natural splitting would identify f∗f^*f∗ with Hom⁡(f∗,Z)⊕Ext⁡(f∗,Z)\Hom(f_*,\ZZ)\oplus\Ext(f_*,\ZZ)Hom(f∗​,Z)⊕Ext(f∗​,Z). Each summand is a functor evaluated at f∗f_*f∗​; so if f∗f_*f∗​ vanishes in all positive degrees, both summands vanish, and therefore so does f∗f^*f∗.□

So it suffices to exhibit one map that kills reduced homology and does not kill reduced cohomology. The smallest such map is already available.

Example 6.8 (A map that homology cannot see). Let q ⁣:RP2→RP2/RP1≅S2q\colon\RP^2\to\RP^2/\RP^1\cong S^2q:RP2→RP2/RP1≅S2 collapse the 111-skeleton.

On homology qqq is zero. By Example 4.7, H~∗(RP2)\widetilde H_*(\RP^2)H∗​(RP2) is Z/2\ZZ/2Z/2 in degree 111 and 000 elsewhere, while H~∗(S2)\widetilde H_*(S^2)H∗​(S2) is Z\ZZZ in degree 222 and 000 elsewhere. In degree 111 the target is 000; in degree 222 the source is 000. So q∗=0q_*=0q∗​=0 in every positive degree, for want of anywhere to go.

On cohomology qqq is not zero. On cellular chains, qqq carries the 222-cell of RP2\RP^2RP2 homeomorphically to the 222-cell of S2S^2S2, so q# ⁣:C2(RP2)→C2(S2)q_\#\colon C_2(\RP^2)\to C_2(S^2)q#​:C2​(RP2)→C2​(S2) is an isomorphism, and therefore so is q# ⁣:C2(S2)→C2(RP2)q^\#\colon C^2(S^2)\to C^2(\RP^2)q#:C2(S2)→C2(RP2). Remark 6.12 (What to do with the mechanism). Example 6.8 is the smallest instance of a general construction: take a space whose reduced homology is a single torsion group, collapse the part of it carrying the torsion, and compare. Exercise 6.6 has already computed the cohomology of M(Z/m,n)M(\ZZ/m,n)M(Z/m,n) for every mmm and nnn, and Proposition 6.4 supplies the collapse map M(Z/m,n)→Sn+1M(\ZZ/m,n)\to S^{n+1}M(Z/m,n)→Sn+1; running the argument of Example 6.8 in those dimensions, and then running it backwards along the inclusion of the bottom sphere, is the natural way to consolidate this section.

Remark 6.13 (Forward). The same phenomenon occurs one theorem later. The Künneth sequence for H∗(X×Y)H_*(X\times Y)H∗​(X×Y) is natural and splits, and its splitting is not natural either; Chapter 8 treats it, and by then the reader should be able to predict both the statement and the shape of the counterexample before reading them.

The deeper point is that the Ext⁡\ExtExt summand carries information about XXX that H∗(X)H_*(X)H∗​(X) alone does not determine as a functor. Recovering it systematically — finding invariants of a space finer than its cohomology groups — is what cohomology operations do, and Chapter 18 constructs them.

6.4 What to carry forward

  • HnH^nHn is Hom⁡(Hn,G)\Hom(H_n,G)Hom(Hn​,G) plus Ext⁡(Hn−1,G)\Ext(H_{n-1},G)Ext(Hn−1​,G): torsion moves up one degree (Theorem 6.1 and Example 6.2).

  • Moore spaces exist for every abelian group, with cells in two dimensions only (Proposition 6.4 and Remark 6.5); they are the standard probes.

  • Natural sequence, natural evaluation map, unnatural splitting (Warning 6.10); the test for non-naturality is Proposition 6.7, and the smallest witness is Example 6.8.

  • Cohomology distinguishes maps that homology cannot — the first evidence that the extra structure on H∗H^*H∗ is worth having, which Chapter 7 turns into a product.
