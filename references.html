How this list is organised

The first section lists the standard references, grouped by the part of this book they support, with a note on what each is good for. The second collects the results this book states without proving, so that each has a precise home. The third records the sources against which the exercise programme was designed, as required of any book whose problems are built from the problems of others.

Locators — page, section or exercise numbers — are given only where they have been checked against the source. Where a result is cited at the level of a whole book, that is deliberate: an unverified locator is worse than none, since it sends a reader to the wrong place with false confidence.

Standard references

Parts I and II: foundations and machinery

A. Hatcher, Algebraic Topology, Cambridge University Press, 2002. The default reference for everything in Parts I and II: van Kampen, covering spaces, cellular homology, universal coefficients, Künneth, cup products, Poincaré duality, Whitehead’s theorems, Postnikov towers, obstruction theory and the Steenrod squares. Chapters 0–3 cover Part I of this book almost exactly; Chapter 4 and the additional sections 4.L and 3.H cover most of Part II. Freely posted by the author at
https://pi.math.cornell.edu/~hatcher/AT/ATpage.html

J. P. May, A Concise Course in Algebraic Topology, University of Chicago Press, 1999. Terse and structural where Hatcher is discursive and geometric. The treatment of cofibrations and the homotopy extension property (Chapter 6) is the one to read alongside Chapter 11; the chapters on Postnikov towers and on characteristic classes are correspondingly compressed. Freely posted at
https://math.uchicago.edu/~may/CONCISE/ConciseRevised.pdf

J. W. Milnor and J. D. Stasheff, Characteristic Classes, Annals of Mathematics Studies 76, Princeton University Press, 1974. The reference for Chapters 12 and 26: Stiefel–Whitney classes and their axioms, the Whitney formula, Chern classes, the Thom isomorphism, the Euler class, and the Wu formula.

A. Hatcher, Vector Bundles and K-Theory, version 2.2, 2017; a book in progress. Complementary to Milnor–Stasheff, and closer in style to this book. Freely posted at
https://pi.math.cornell.edu/~hatcher/VBKT/VBpage.html

Part III: spectral sequences and operations

A. Hatcher, Spectral Sequences in Algebraic Topology; an unpublished manuscript. The reference for Chapters 15 and 16: its first chapter is the best available account of how one actually runs a Serre spectral sequence, as opposed to how one constructs it. Freely posted at
https://pi.math.cornell.edu/~hatcher/SSAT/SSATpage.html

N. E. Steenrod and D. B. A. Epstein, Cohomology Operations, Annals of Mathematics Studies 50, Princeton University Press, 1962. The construction of the squares, the Adem relations and the admissible basis of Chapters 18 and 27.

Part IV: abstract homotopy theory

E. Riehl, Categorical Homotopy Theory, New Mathematical Monographs 24, Cambridge University Press, 2014. The reference for most of Part IV: homotopy limits and colimits, derived functors as Kan extensions, model structures, simplicial sets, quasi-categories, the homotopy coherent nerve and its left adjoint. Chapters 20, 21, 22, 23 and 29 all follow its treatment in outline. Freely posted at
https://emilyriehl.github.io/files/cathtpy.pdf

S. Mac Lane, Categories for the Working Mathematician, 2nd edition, Graduate Texts in Mathematics 5, Springer, 1998. Adjunctions, Kan extensions, ends and coends: the background Chapter 20 assumes.

M. Hovey, Model Categories, Mathematical Surveys and Monographs 63, American Mathematical Society, 1999; and D. G. Quillen, Homotopical Algebra, Lecture Notes in Mathematics 43, Springer, 1967. The axioms of Chapter 22 and their consequences, in the modern and the original treatments respectively.

P. G. Goerss and J. F. Jardine, Simplicial Homotopy Theory, Progress in Mathematics 174, Birkhäuser, 1999. Simplicial sets in detail, including the proof that simplicial groups are Kan complexes (Theorem 23.5) and the Dold–Kan correspondence.

J. Lurie, Higher Topos Theory, Annals of Mathematics Studies 170, Princeton University Press, 2009. The systematic development of quasi-categories, well beyond Chapters 23 and 29.

Y. Félix, S. Halperin and J.-C. Thomas, Rational Homotopy Theory, Graduate Texts in Mathematics 205, Springer, 2001. The standard reference for Chapters 24, 25 and 31: Sullivan models, minimal models, spatial realisation, the homotopy Lie algebra, and the elliptic–hyperbolic dichotomy.

A. Berglund, Rational Homotopy Theory, lecture notes, Stockholm University, 2012. A shorter route to the same material. Posted at
https://staff.math.su.se/alexb/rathom2.pdf

Part V: research preparation

J.-L. Loday and B. Vallette, Algebraic Operads, Grundlehren der mathematischen Wissenschaften 346, Springer, 2012. The reference for Chapter 30: S\mathbb{S}S-modules, the composition product, Koszul duality, twisting morphisms, and the homotopy transfer theorem of Theorem 31.7. Freely posted at
https://www.math.univ-paris13.fr/~vallette/Operads.pdf

S. Schwede, Symmetric Spectra, version 3.0, 2012; a preliminary book draft. The reference for Chapter 28, including the pathologies of naive homotopy groups that Warning 28.5 describes. The document states on its first page that it is incomplete, so its numbering is not stable between versions. Freely posted at
https://www.math.uni-bonn.de/people/schwede/SymSpec-v3.pdf

M. Hovey, B. Shipley and J. Smith, Symmetric spectra, Journal of the American Mathematical Society 13 (2000), 149–208. The original construction.

Results quoted without proof

This book states a number of theorems it does not prove. Most are standard and covered by the references above — van Kampen, excision, Poincaré duality, the construction of the Serre spectral sequence, the existence of minimal models. The following are the ones where the omission is substantial, and each is flagged in the text at the point of use.

  • The homotopy classification of lens spaces (Example 7.10 and Warning 7.11). Classical, and due to J. H. C. Whitehead; the invariant is the linking form, and the surrounding theory is simple-homotopy theory. See M. M. Cohen, A Course in Simple-Homotopy Theory, Graduate Texts in Mathematics 10, Springer, 1973, and J. W. Milnor, Whitehead torsion, Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society 72 (1966), 358–426.

  • Serre’s computation of π∗(Sn)⊗Q\pi_*(S^n)\otimes\QQπ∗​(Sn)⊗Q (Theorem 19.10 and Warning 19.11). J.-P. Serre, Groupes d’homotopie et classes de groupes abéliens, Annals of Mathematics 58 (1953), 258–294. Hatcher’s spectral sequences manuscript gives a modern account; Chapter 24 obtains the same answer by another route.

  • Adams’ theorem on Hopf invariant one (Remarks 16.17 and 27.11). J. F. Adams, On the non-existence of elements of Hopf invariant one, Annals of Mathematics 72 (1960), 20–104. The later KKK-theoretic proof is due to Adams and Atiyah, K-theory and the Hopf invariant, Quarterly Journal of Mathematics (Oxford), 1966. Chapter 27 proves the elementary part — that the dimension must be a power of two.

  • Milnor–Moore (Theorem 25.7). J. W. Milnor and J. C. Moore, On the structure of Hopf algebras, Annals of Mathematics 81 (1965), 211–264.

  • Bott’s integrality theorem (Remark 26.11), constraining which total Chern classes occur. See Milnor–Stasheff.

  • Higher Massey products and formality (Remark 31.11). The higher products are due to D. Kraines, Massey higher products, Transactions of the American Mathematical Society 124 (1966), 431–449; that formality forces them to vanish, and that the converse fails, is discussed in P. Deligne, P. Griffiths, J. Morgan and D. Sullivan, Real homotopy theory of Kähler manifolds, Inventiones Mathematicae 29 (1975), 245–274.

Sources of inspiration, and originality

This book was written to prepare a reader for a separate collection of exercises, whose problems are drawn from the eight sources listed above — Hatcher’s three books, May, Berglund, Riehl, Schwede, and Loday–Vallette. Those problems shaped this book: they determined which techniques are developed, in what order, and to what depth. In that sense the exercise sets of those texts are the source of inspiration for everything in these pages, and the debt is acknowledged here rather than left implicit.

What the book does not do is reproduce them. No exercise in this book is an exercise from any of those sources, reworded or otherwise. Where a source exercise identified a technique worth teaching, the book develops the technique and then poses a different problem using it — usually on a different space, in a different dimension, or from the opposite direction. Each chapter’s source file opens with a comment recording precisely which problems it was written to prepare for and which it deliberately leaves alone; those comments are part of the manuscript and are not printed.

Three practices support that claim. First, the general theorems of the subject are proved here as theorems, not smuggled in as exercises: a reader is never asked to rediscover van Kampen or the Adem relations. Second, where the book would otherwise state a target result outright, it states the ingredients and names what remains — the passages beginning “the natural exercise here” are that device, and they are deliberate rather than evasive. Third, the manuscript is checked mechanically against the source problems: a script compares every printed sentence of this book with the statements, hints and solutions of all eighty-four, and reports any run of ten consecutive words in common, with mathematical notation excluded from the comparison so that shared symbols cannot trigger or mask a match. At the time of writing it reports one accepted collision, the statement of the Wang sequence, which is a standard theorem this book is obliged to state.

A note on licences. Six of the eight sources are posted freely by their authors, and two are also commercially published. Free availability is not permission to reproduce, and nothing in this book relies on any such permission: the material here is either standard mathematics stated in the author’s own words, or original exposition and problems. Readers preparing their own courses from these sources should observe the same distinction.
