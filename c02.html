Chapter 2

Presentations from gluing data: van Kampen and CW structures

In this chapter

  1. 2.1 From cells to generators and relations
  2. 2.2 Mapping tori
  3. 2.3 Retractions, and how to rule them out
  4. 2.4 Identification complexes in dimension three
  5. 2.5 What to carry forward

A space presented by gluing instructions — a polygon with edge identifications, a cube with face identifications, a cylinder with its ends matched by a map — has a fundamental group presented by generators and relations, and the dictionary between the two presentations is mechanical. This chapter sets up that dictionary and then does what a first course usually has no time for: uses it on spaces where the answer is not obvious, and uses it backwards, to prove that certain maps cannot exist.

The mechanical part is worth internalising because it is the only computational access to π1\pi_1π1​ we have. The subtle part is that the dictionary is not injective: several gluings give the same group, and π1\pi_1π1​ will turn out not to determine the space. The chapter ends there, and Chapters 4 and 7 supply the finer invariants.

2.1 From cells to generators and relations

We freely use the van Kampen theorem. What we need in practice is its specialisation to CW complexes, and one normalisation.

Proposition 2.1. Every connected CW complex is homotopy equivalent to a CW complex with a single 000-cell.

For a graph this is the familiar move of collapsing a spanning tree; in general one collapses a spanning tree of the 111-skeleton. The justification — that collapsing a contractible subcomplex does not change the homotopy type — is a consequence of the homotopy extension property, and we prove it in Chapter 11. We use it now and pay for it later.

Proposition 2.2. Let XXX be a connected CW complex with a single 000-cell ppp, with 111-cells (ai)i∈I(a_i)_{i\in I}(ai​)i∈I​ and 222-cells attached along loops wjw_jwj​, j∈Jj\in Jj∈J. Then

π1(X,p)  ≅  ⟨ ai (i∈I)  ∣  wj (j∈J) ⟩,\pi_1(X,p)\;\cong\;\big\langle\,a_i\ (i\in I)\;\big|\;w_j\ (j\in J)\,\big\rangle,π1​(X,p)≅⟨ai​ (i∈I)​wj​ (j∈J)⟩,

where wjw_jwj​ is read as a word in the ai±1a_i^{\pm1}ai±1​ by recording the 111-cells the attaching map traverses.

Proof sketch. The 111-skeleton is a wedge of circles, with free fundamental group on the aia_iai​. Attaching a 222-cell along www kills exactly the normal closure of [w][w][w], by van Kampen applied to the union of X1X^1X1 (thickened) with the open cell. Cells of dimension ≥3\ge3≥3 do not change π1\pi_1π1​, again by van Kampen, because the attaching spheres are simply connected.□

Remark 2.3. There is a small subtlety hidden in the phrase “read as a word”. An attaching map S1→X1S^1\to X^1S1→X1 is a free loop: it comes with no basepoint, so its class in π1(X1,p)\pi_1(X^1,p)π1​(X1,p) is only well defined up to conjugation, and up to inversion if one reverses the orientation. This does no harm, because the normal closure of [w][w][w] is unchanged by conjugating www. It does mean that a relator is only ever well defined up to conjugacy — which is the reason presentations coming from geometry so often look different from the ones in a group theory book. Compare Exercise 1.15: whether a relator is trivial does not depend on the basepoint either.

2.2 Mapping tori

Definition 2.5. Let f ⁣:X→Xf\colon X\to Xf:X→X be a map. The mapping torus is

Tf=(X×[0,1])/((x,0)∼(f(x),1)).T_f=\big(X\times[0,1]\big)\big/\big((x,0)\sim(f(x),1)\big).Tf​=(X×[0,1])/((x,0)∼(f(x),1)).

We fix this convention once and never change it: the bottom of the cylinder is glued into the top by fff. Exercise 2.11 asks the reader to redo the computation with the roles exchanged, precisely so that the dependence is seen rather than feared.

2.3 Retractions, and how to rule them out

The presentations above are useful in the negative direction too. The basic principle occupies one line.

Lemma 2.13. If A⊆XA\subseteq XA⊆X and r ⁣:X→Ar\colon X\to Ar:X→A is a retraction, then i∗ ⁣:π1(A,a)→π1(X,a)i_*\colon\pi_1(A,a)\to\pi_1(X,a)i∗​:π1​(A,a)→π1​(X,a) is injective and split: there is a homomorphism r∗r_*r∗​ with r∗i∗=idr_*i_*=\mathrm{id}r∗​i∗​=id. The same holds after abelianising.

Proof. Apply π1\pi_1π1​ to r∘i=idAr\circ i=\mathrm{id}_Ar∘i=idA​; functoriality gives r∗i∗=idr_*i_*=\mathrm{id}r∗​i∗​=id, and abelianisation is a functor.□

Injectivity alone is a weak conclusion, and the temptation is to look for its failure. Usually it does not fail; what fails is splitting.

Exercise 2.14 (medium). Let MMM be the Möbius band and ∂M\partial M∂M its boundary circle. Show that π1(∂M)→π1(M)\pi_1(\partial M)\to\pi_1(M)π1​(∂M)→π1​(M) is injective, but that MMM does not retract onto ∂M\partial M∂M.

Solution. The deformation retraction onto the core gives π1(M)=Z⟨c⟩\pi_1(M)=\ZZ\langle c\rangleπ1​(M)=Z⟨c⟩, and the boundary circle runs twice around the core, so i∗ ⁣:Z→Zi_*\colon\ZZ\to\ZZi∗​:Z→Z is multiplication by 222. That is injective, so no injectivity obstruction exists.

But a retraction would give r∗ ⁣:Z→Zr_*\colon\ZZ\to\ZZr∗​:Z→Z with r∗(2x)=xr_*(2x)=xr∗​(2x)=x for all xxx; taking x=1x=1x=1 forces 2r∗(1)=12r_*(1)=12r∗​(1)=1 in Z\ZZZ, which is impossible. Hence there is no retraction.

The positive direction needs a way to turn a homomorphism into a map. For targets that are aspherical this is possible, and for S1S^1S1 it is elementary.

Proposition 2.15. Let XXX be a connected CW complex with basepoint ppp. Every homomorphism φ ⁣:π1(X,p)→Z\varphi\colon\pi_1(X,p)\to\ZZφ:π1​(X,p)→Z is induced by a map g ⁣:X→S1g\colon X\to S^1g:X→S1.

Proof sketch. Normalise XXX to have a single 000-cell (Proposition 2.1) and send it to the basepoint of S1S^1S1. Send the 111-cell aia_iai​ to a loop of degree φ(ai)\varphi(a_i)φ(ai​). Over a 222-cell with attaching word wjw_jwj​, the composite S1→X1→S1S^1\to X^1\to S^1S1→X1→S1 has degree φ(wj)=0\varphi(w_j)=0φ(wj​)=0 because φ\varphiφ is a homomorphism killing the relators; a degree-zero self-map of S1S^1S1 is nullhomotopic, so the map extends over the cell. For cells of dimension n≥3n\ge3n≥3 the obstruction to extending lies in πn−1(S1)=0\pi_{n-1}(S^1)=0πn−1​(S1)=0, so the extension continues.□

Remark 2.16. The last two sentences of that proof are obstruction theory in embryo: extend skeleton by skeleton, and at each stage the obstruction is a homotopy class of the attaching map composed with what is already built. Chapter 11 makes the first half precise (the extension lemma) and Chapter 17 makes all of it precise, with the obstructions organised into cohomology classes.

2.4 Identification complexes in dimension three

The dictionary of Proposition 2.2 applies verbatim to solids with identified faces, provided one first does the bookkeeping: which vertices become the same point, which edges become the same edge. That step is where the errors happen, and it is worth doing slowly on an example where the answer is known.

Example 2.19 (The three-torus). Let XXX be the cube I3I^3I3 with each face identified with the opposite face by the perpendicular translation.

Vertices. The translations identify all eight vertices to one point: the three translations generate a group acting transitively on the vertices of the cube modulo the identifications, and following any vertex under the three translations reaches every other. So there is one 000-cell.

Edges. The twelve edges fall into three orbits of four, one for each coordinate direction: the four edges parallel to the xxx-axis are identified by the yyy- and zzz-translations. Call the resulting 111-cells aaa, bbb, ccc.

Faces. The six faces are identified in opposite pairs, leaving three 222-cells. The face perpendicular to the zzz-axis is a square whose boundary reads aba−1b−1aba^{-1}b^{-1}aba−1b−1, and similarly for the others.

So XXX has one 000-cell, three 111-cells, three 222-cells and one 333-cell, and Proposition 2.2 gives π1(X)=⟨a,b,c∣[a,b],[b,c],[a,c]⟩≅Z3\pi_1(X)=\langle a,b,c\mid[a,b],[b,c],[a,c]\rangle\cong\ZZ^3π1​(X)=⟨a,b,c∣[a,b],[b,c],[a,c]⟩≅Z3, as it must, since X=S1×S1×S1X=S^1\times S^1\times S^1X=S1×S1×S1.

Remark 2.20. Two features of Example 2.19 are accidents of the translations. First, all vertices became one point; second, each edge orbit had exactly four members. Compose the identifications with a rotation of the face and both counts can change: a rotation carries an edge of one face to a different edge of the opposite face, so the edge orbits are permuted in longer cycles and the vertex orbits can split. Any such computation must therefore begin by listing the vertex orbits and then the edge orbits, in that order, before a single relation is written down. Example 2.21 is a first case where the answer is not Zn\ZZ^nZn.

Exercise 2.22 (medium). Verify the cell counts in the ball model of L(p,q)L(p,q)L(p,q): the boundary sphere becomes one 222-cell whose attaching map runs ppp times around a circle built from one 000-cell and one 111-cell. Deduce the presentation π1=⟨a∣ap⟩\pi_1=\langle a\mid a^p\rangleπ1​=⟨a∣ap⟩ directly, without using the covering space.

Hint. The rotation by 2πq/p2\pi q/p2πq/p has order ppp on the equator, so the equator’s marked points fall into orbits of size ppp; a single 111-cell survives, and the boundary of the 222-cell winds around it once for each of the ppp sectors.

Solution. Mark ppp equally spaced points on the equator of D3D^3D3, cutting it into ppp arcs. The gluing rotates by 2πq/p2\pi q/p2πq/p, which permutes the marked points cyclically and permutes the arcs cyclically; so all ppp marked points become a single 000-cell and all ppp arcs a single 111-cell aaa. The upper hemisphere is glued to the lower, so the two hemispheres become a single 222-cell, and its attaching map runs once along each of the ppp arcs, i.e. traverses aaa exactly ppp times. The interior of the ball is the 333-cell. Proposition 2.2 now gives ⟨a∣ap⟩=Z/p\langle a\mid a^p\rangle=\ZZ/p⟨a∣ap⟩=Z/p, agreeing with Example 2.21.

Remark 2.23 (Where this chapter stops). The lens spaces expose the limit of the method. For fixed ppp, every L(p,q)L(p,q)L(p,q) has fundamental group Z/p\ZZ/pZ/p, so π1\pi_1π1​ cannot tell them apart. Nor can homology: we will compute in Chapter 4 that H∗(L(p,q))H_*(L(p,q))H∗​(L(p,q)) is also independent of qqq. Yet they are genuinely different spaces — L(5,1)L(5,1)L(5,1) and L(5,2)L(5,2)L(5,2) are not even homotopy equivalent. Detecting that needs a product structure rather than a group structure, and we return to it in Chapter 7.

This is the first instance of the pattern that runs through the book: build an invariant, compute with it until it fails, and let the failure name the next invariant.
