Chapter 14

Fibrations and the first homotopy groups of spheres

In this chapter

  1. 14.1 The long exact sequence
  2. 14.2 When the sequence splits
  3. 14.3 First computations
  4. 14.4 What to carry forward

A fibration converts a space into two simpler ones at the cost of a long exact sequence, and that trade is the only systematic access to higher homotopy groups we have. This chapter sets up the sequence, isolates the mechanism by which it splits, and runs it on the two bundles where the answer is a theorem rather than a tautology.

Everything here is preparation for Chapter 15, which replaces the long exact sequence by a spectral sequence and thereby handles fibrations whose fibre is not a sphere.

14.1 The long exact sequence

Definition 14.1. A map p ⁣:E→Bp\colon E\to Bp:E→B is a fibration if it has the homotopy lifting property: every homotopy X×I→BX\times I\to BX×I→B whose restriction to X×{0}X\times\{0\}X×{0} lifts to EEE lifts entirely.

Fibre bundles over paracompact bases are fibrations, and that is how almost every example arises.

Theorem 14.2 (Relative homotopy; recalled). Let p ⁣:E→Bp\colon E\to Bp:E→B be a fibration with BBB path-connected, b0∈Bb_0\in Bb0​∈B, F=p−1(b0)F=p^{-1}(b_0)F=p−1(b0​) and x0∈Fx_0\in Fx0​∈F. Then

p∗ ⁣:πn(E,F,x0)⟶πn(B,b0)p_*\colon\pi_n(E,F,x_0)\longrightarrow\pi_n(B,b_0)p∗​:πn​(E,F,x0​)⟶πn​(B,b0​)

is an isomorphism for all n≥1n\ge1n≥1.

Corollary 14.3 (The long exact sequence).

⋯→πn(F)→ i∗ πn(E)→ p∗ πn(B)→ ∂ πn−1(F)→⋯→π0(E).\cdots\to\pi_n(F)\xrightarrow{\ i_*\ }\pi_n(E)\xrightarrow{\ p_*\ }\pi_n(B) \xrightarrow{\ \partial\ }\pi_{n-1}(F)\to\cdots\to\pi_0(E).⋯→πn​(F) i∗​ ​πn​(E) p∗​ ​πn​(B) ∂ ​πn−1​(F)→⋯→π0​(E).

Proof. Substitute Theorem 14.2 into the long exact sequence of the pair (E,F)(E,F)(E,F).□

Exercise 14.4 (easy). Let F→E→BF\to E\to BF→E→B be a fibration with EEE contractible. Show πn(B)≅πn−1(F)\pi_n(B)\cong\pi_{n-1}(F)πn​(B)≅πn−1​(F) for all n≥1n\ge1n≥1.

Solution. In Corollary 14.3 the terms πn(E)\pi_n(E)πn​(E) and πn−1(E)\pi_{n-1}(E)πn−1​(E) vanish, so exactness of πn(E)→πn(B)→∂πn−1(F)→πn−1(E)\pi_n(E)\to\pi_n(B)\xrightarrow{\partial}\pi_{n-1}(F)\to\pi_{n-1}(E)πn​(E)→πn​(B)∂​πn−1​(F)→πn−1​(E) forces ∂\partial∂ to be injective and surjective.

Example 14.5 (Path–loop). For any based space BBB the path space PBPBPB of paths starting at b0b_0b0​ is contractible, and evaluation at the endpoint is a fibration PB→BPB\to BPB→B with fibre ΩB\Omega BΩB. By Exercise 14.4,

πn(B)≅πn−1(ΩB)(n≥1),\pi_n(B)\cong\pi_{n-1}(\Omega B)\qquad(n\ge1),πn​(B)≅πn−1​(ΩB)(n≥1),

which is the statement that looping shifts homotopy groups down by one.

Exercise 14.6 (medium). The unit sphere S∞⊆C∞S^\infty\subseteq\CC^\inftyS∞⊆C∞ is contractible, and the circle acts freely on it with quotient CP∞\CP^\inftyCP∞. Deduce that CP∞\CP^\inftyCP∞ is a K(Z,2)K(\ZZ,2)K(Z,2).

Hint. A free action of a compact Lie group gives a fibre bundle, hence a fibration, with fibre the group.

Solution. The action gives a fibre bundle S1→S∞→CP∞S^1\to S^\infty\to\CP^\inftyS1→S∞→CP∞, hence a fibration. Since S∞S^\inftyS∞ is contractible, Exercise 14.4 gives

πn(CP∞)≅πn−1(S1).\pi_n(\CP^\infty)\cong\pi_{n-1}(S^1).πn​(CP∞)≅πn−1​(S1).

Now π0(S1)=0\pi_0(S^1)=0π0​(S1)=0, π1(S1)=Z\pi_1(S^1)=\ZZπ1​(S1)=Z and πk(S1)=0\pi_k(S^1)=0πk​(S1)=0 for k≥2k\ge2k≥2 — the last because the universal cover R\RRR is contractible and covers induce isomorphisms on πk\pi_kπk​ for k≥2k\ge2k≥2 (Exercise 13.7). So π2(CP∞)=Z\pi_2(\CP^\infty)=\ZZπ2​(CP∞)=Z and all other homotopy groups vanish: CP∞\CP^\inftyCP∞ is a K(Z,2)K(\ZZ,2)K(Z,2).

14.2 When the sequence splits

A long exact sequence is most useful when it breaks into short pieces, and there are two standard hypotheses that make it do so. One is easy and is below; the other is harder and is the point of Remark 14.9.

Exercise 14.7 (medium). Suppose the fibration F→E→pBF\to E\xrightarrow{p}BF→Ep​B admits a section, i.e. a map s ⁣:B→Es\colon B\to Es:B→E with p∘s=idBp\circ s=\mathrm{id}_Bp∘s=idB​. Show that for n≥2n\ge2n≥2

πn(E)≅πn(F)⊕πn(B).\pi_n(E)\cong\pi_n(F)\oplus\pi_n(B).πn​(E)≅πn​(F)⊕πn​(B).

Hint. p∗s∗=idp_*s_*=\mathrm{id}p∗​s∗​=id already gives a splitting of one map in Corollary 14.3. What does that force ∂\partial∂ to be?

Solution. From p∘s=idp\circ s=\mathrm{id}p∘s=id we get p∗s∗=idp_*s_*=\mathrm{id}p∗​s∗​=id on πn(B)\pi_n(B)πn​(B), so p∗p_*p∗​ is surjective with a chosen splitting s∗s_*s∗​. Exactness at πn(B)\pi_n(B)πn​(B) then makes ∂=0\partial=0∂=0, so the sequence breaks into

0→πn(F)→ i∗ πn(E)→ p∗ πn(B)→0,0\to\pi_n(F)\xrightarrow{\ i_*\ }\pi_n(E)\xrightarrow{\ p_*\ }\pi_n(B)\to0 ,0→πn​(F) i∗​ ​πn​(E) p∗​ ​πn​(B)→0,

exact because ∂\partial∂ vanishes on both sides. For n≥2n\ge2n≥2 these are abelian groups and the sequence splits via s∗s_*s∗​, giving πn(E)≅πn(F)⊕πn(B)\pi_n(E)\cong\pi_n(F)\oplus\pi_n(B)πn​(E)≅πn​(F)⊕πn​(B).

Warning 14.8 (Surjective does not mean split). A short exact sequence of abelian groups splits when the right-hand term is free, and otherwise only when a splitting is produced. In Exercise 14.7 the splitting came from the section; with no section in sight, exhibiting one is work, and asserting it because a map happens to be surjective is an error.

Remark 14.9 (The other splitting mechanism). The dual hypothesis to a section is that the fibre inclusion is nullhomotopic. It also collapses Corollary 14.3 into short exact sequences — i∗=0i_*=0i∗​=0 makes p∗p_*p∗​ injective and ∂\partial∂ surjective — but now no splitting is handed over, and by Warning 14.8 one has to be built.

The construction is worth naming, because it is used again in Chapter 17. A nullhomotopy of a map g ⁣:A→Eg\colon A\to Eg:A→E is exactly a map CA→ECA\to ECA→E out of the cone extending ggg. So if α ⁣:Sn−1→F\alpha\colon S^{n-1}\to Fα:Sn−1→F is given and the inclusion F↪EF\hookrightarrow EF↪E is nullhomotopic, composing the nullhomotopy with α\alphaα produces a map of pairs

(Dn,Sn−1)⟶(E,F),\big(D^{n},S^{n-1}\big)\longrightarrow(E,F),(Dn,Sn−1)⟶(E,F),

that is, a class in πn(E,F)\pi_n(E,F)πn​(E,F) — which Theorem 14.2 identifies with πn(B)\pi_n(B)πn​(B). Checking that this is a homomorphism, and that it is a section of ∂\partial∂, is the natural exercise here, and it turns a structural hypothesis about the fibre into a direct sum decomposition of the homotopy groups of the base.

14.3 First computations

Example 14.10 (The Hopf bundle). The Hopf map p ⁣:S3→S2p\colon S^3\to S^2p:S3→S2, (z1,z2)↦[z1:z2](z_1,z_2)\mapsto[z_1:z_2](z1​,z2​)↦[z1​:z2​], is a fibre bundle with fibre S1S^1S1. Since πn(S1)=0\pi_n(S^1)=0πn​(S1)=0 for n≥2n\ge2n≥2, the long exact sequence

πn(S1)→πn(S3)→πn(S2)→πn−1(S1)\pi_n(S^1)\to\pi_n(S^3)\to\pi_n(S^2)\to\pi_{n-1}(S^1)πn​(S1)→πn​(S3)→πn​(S2)→πn−1​(S1)

has vanishing outer terms for n≥3n\ge3n≥3, giving

πn(S3)≅πn(S2)(n≥3).\pi_n(S^3)\cong\pi_n(S^2)\qquad(n\ge3).πn​(S3)≅πn​(S2)(n≥3).

In particular π3(S2)≅π3(S3)=Z\pi_3(S^2)\cong\pi_3(S^3)=\ZZπ3​(S2)≅π3​(S3)=Z, generated by the Hopf map itself.

That is already a surprise worth registering: S2S^2S2 has nontrivial homotopy in a degree above its dimension, and it has infinitely many distinct homotopy classes of maps from S3S^3S3, while H3(S2)=0H_3(S^2)=0H3​(S2)=0 sees none of them.

Exercise 14.11 (easy). Show that πn(Tk)=0\pi_n(T^k)=0πn​(Tk)=0 for all n≥2n\ge2n≥2, and more generally that a space with contractible universal cover has vanishing higher homotopy groups.

Solution. The universal cover of TkT^kTk is Rk\RR^kRk, which is contractible. By Exercise 13.7, πn(Tk)≅πn(Rk)=0\pi_n(T^k)\cong\pi_n(\RR^k)=0πn​(Tk)≅πn​(Rk)=0 for n≥2n\ge2n≥2. The same argument applies whenever the universal cover is contractible; such a space is a K(π1,1)K(\pi_1,1)K(π1​,1).

Theorem 14.12 (Freudenthal; recalled). The suspension map πn(Sk)→πn+1(Sk+1)\pi_n(S^k)\to\pi_{n+1}(S^{k+1})πn​(Sk)→πn+1​(Sk+1) is an isomorphism for n<2k−1n<2k-1n<2k−1 and a surjection for n=2k−1n=2k-1n=2k−1.

Example 14.13 (The stable range). Freudenthal makes πn+k(Sk)\pi_{n+k}(S^k)πn+k​(Sk) independent of kkk once k>n+1k>n+1k>n+1; the common value is the nnn-th stable stem πns\pi_n^sπns​. The first few are

π0s=Z,π1s=Z/2,π2s=Z/2,π3s=Z/24.\pi_0^s=\ZZ,\qquad \pi_1^s=\ZZ/2,\qquad \pi_2^s=\ZZ/2,\qquad \pi_3^s=\ZZ/24 .π0s​=Z,π1s​=Z/2,π2s​=Z/2,π3s​=Z/24.

Outside the stable range the groups are wilder and depend on kkk; the computation of Example 14.10 is unstable, π3(S2)\pi_3(S^2)π3​(S2) living well below the range where Freudenthal applies.

Remark 14.14 (What the long exact sequence cannot do). Example 14.10 worked because the fibre was S1S^1S1, whose homotopy groups vanish above degree one, so two of every three terms in the sequence were zero. That is a special circumstance. For a fibration with fibre S3S^3S3, say, the outer terms do not vanish and the sequence determines its middle terms only up to extension — and in general not even that, since the maps are unknown.

There are two ways forward, and the book takes both. Chapter 17 keeps track of the ambiguity by organising it into cohomology classes. Chapter 15 replaces the long exact sequence by a spectral sequence, which computes the homology of the total space from that of the base and the fibre whatever they are, at the price of differentials that must themselves be determined.

Before leaving the long exact sequence it is worth noticing what it can do when a structural hypothesis is available: Exercise 14.7 and Remark 14.9 each convert a single geometric statement into a direct sum decomposition, and applied to the classical Hopf bundles S3→S7→S4S^3\to S^7\to S^4S3→S7→S4 and S7→S15→S8S^7\to S^{15}\to S^8S7→S15→S8 they produce infinite cyclic summands in homotopy groups of spheres that no elementary argument reaches.

14.4 What to carry forward

  • πn(E,F)≅πn(B)\pi_n(E,F)\cong\pi_n(B)πn​(E,F)≅πn​(B) for a fibration (Theorem 14.2) — the source of the long exact sequence and of every splitting argument.

  • Contractible total space turns the connecting map into an isomorphism (Exercise 14.4); this computes Ω\OmegaΩ and identifies CP∞=K(Z,2)\CP^\infty=K(\ZZ,2)CP∞=K(Z,2) (Example 14.5 and Exercise 14.6).

  • A section splits the sequence (Exercise 14.7); a nullhomotopic fibre inclusion does too, but the splitting must be constructed from a cone (Warning 14.8 and Remark 14.9).

  • π3(S2)=Z\pi_3(S^2)=\ZZπ3​(S2)=Z (Example 14.10); homotopy above the dimension is the normal state of affairs, and homology sees none of it.

  • Freudenthal and the stable range (Theorem 14.12 and Example 14.13).
