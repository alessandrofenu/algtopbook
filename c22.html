Chapter 21 repaired individual constructions one at a time. A model structure repairs all of them at once: it is a package of three classes of maps from which homotopy limits, homotopy colimits, derived functors and the homotopy category can all be extracted formally.

The axioms look unmotivated on first reading. They are best understood as the minimum needed for two things: that every object can be replaced by a well-behaved one, and that a functor which behaves correctly on the well-behaved objects has a derived functor. Section 22.3 is where that pays off.

Example 22.3. On Set\mathbf{Set}Set, the monomorphisms and the epimorphisms form a weak factorisation system: every function factors through its image as an injection followed by a surjection, and an injection lifts against a surjection because a surjection of sets splits. On Top\mathbf{Top}Top, the relative CW inclusions and the maps with the right lifting property against them — the trivial Serre fibrations — form another.

22.2 The axioms

Definition 22.4. A model structure on a complete and cocomplete category M\mathcal{M}M consists of three classes of maps — weak equivalences W\mathcal{W}W, cofibrations C\mathcal{C}C, fibrations F\mathcal{F}F — such that

  1. W\mathcal{W}W satisfies two-out-of-three: if two of fff, ggg, gfgfgf lie in W\mathcal{W}W then so does the third;

  2. all three classes are closed under retracts;

  3. (C∩W, F)(\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{W},\ \mathcal{F})(C∩W, F) and (C, F∩W)(\mathcal{C},\ \mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{W})(C, F∩W) are weak factorisation systems.

An object is cofibrant if ∅→X\varnothing\to X∅→X is a cofibration, and fibrant if X→∗X\to\astX→∗ is a fibration.

Example 22.5 (Two structures on spaces). Top\mathbf{Top}Top carries at least two model structures.

In the Serre structure the weak equivalences are the weak homotopy equivalences, the fibrations are the Serre fibrations, and the cofibrant objects are the retracts of CW complexes. Every object is fibrant.

In the Hurewicz structure the weak equivalences are the honest homotopy equivalences and the fibrations are the Hurewicz fibrations. Every object is both fibrant and cofibrant.

The two differ, and the reason they differ is Example 22.6.

Example 22.6 (Weak equivalence is weaker). The Warsaw circle — the closed topologist’s sine curve with its two ends joined by an arc — has vanishing homotopy groups in every degree, so the inclusion of a point into it is a weak homotopy equivalence. It is not a homotopy equivalence: the space is not contractible.

So the two classes of Example 22.5 are genuinely different, and Whitehead’s theorem (Theorem 13.1) is precisely the statement that they agree on CW complexes.

Exercise 22.7 (medium). Show that in a model category the cofibrations are closed under cobase change: if i ⁣:A→Bi\colon A\to Bi:A→B is a cofibration and A→A′A\to A'A→A′ is any map, then the induced map A′→A′⊔ABA'\to A'\sqcup_ABA′→A′⊔A​B is a cofibration. Dually, fibrations are closed under base change.

Hint. A cofibration is exactly a map with the left lifting property against every trivial fibration. Given a lifting problem for the pushout, restrict it along A→BA\to BA→B to get one for iii.

Solution. Let p ⁣:X→Yp\colon X\to Yp:X→Y be a trivial fibration and consider a square with left edge A′→A′⊔ABA'\to A'\sqcup_ABA′→A′⊔A​B and right edge ppp. Composing the top edge with B→A′⊔ABB\to A'\sqcup_ABB→A′⊔A​B gives a square with left edge iii and right edge ppp, which by hypothesis has a filler B→XB\to XB→X. That filler agrees on AAA with the composite A→A′→XA\to A'\to XA→A′→X, because both are obtained from the original square, so by the universal property of the pushout the two together define a map A′⊔AB→XA'\sqcup_AB\to XA′⊔A​B→X. It is a filler for the original square, again by the universal property. Hence A′→A′⊔ABA'\to A'\sqcup_ABA′→A′⊔A​B lifts against every trivial fibration and is a cofibration.

The dual statement is the same argument with all arrows reversed.

22.3 Derived functors

A functor between model categories rarely preserves weak equivalences. The repair is to apply it only to well-behaved objects, and the theorem that makes this legitimate is the following.

Theorem 22.8 (Ken Brown’s lemma). Let F ⁣:M→NF\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{N}F:M→N be a functor from a model category to a category with weak equivalences. If FFF sends trivial cofibrations between cofibrant objects to weak equivalences, then it sends all weak equivalences between cofibrant objects to weak equivalences.

Exercise 22.9 (medium). Prove Theorem 22.8.

Hint. Given a weak equivalence f ⁣:A→Bf\colon A\to Bf:A→B between cofibrant objects, factor the map (f,id) ⁣:A⊔B→B(f,\mathrm{id})\colon A\sqcup B\to B(f,id):A⊔B→B as a cofibration followed by a trivial fibration.

Solution. Factor A⊔B→BA\sqcup B\to BA⊔B→B as A⊔B→jC→qBA\sqcup B\xrightarrow{j}C\xrightarrow{q}BA⊔Bj​Cq​B with jjj a cofibration and qqq a trivial fibration. The two inclusions give maps jA ⁣:A→Cj_A\colon A\to CjA​:A→C and jB ⁣:B→Cj_B\colon B\to CjB​:B→C, both cofibrations because AAA and BBB are cofibrant and cofibrations are closed under cobase change. Since qjB=idBqj_B= \mathrm{id}_BqjB​=idB​ is a weak equivalence and qqq is one, two-out-of-three makes jBj_BjB​ a trivial cofibration; likewise qjA=fqj_A=fqjA​=f is a weak equivalence, so jAj_AjA​ is a trivial cofibration. Definition 22.10. The total left derived functor of F ⁣:M→NF\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{N}F:M→N is the left Kan extension of M→N→Ho N\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{N}\to\mathrm{Ho}\,\mathcal{N}M→N→HoN along the localisation M→Ho M\mathcal{M}\to\mathrm{Ho}\,\mathcal{M}M→HoM, written LF\mathbb{L}FLF.

Theorem 22.11 (Existence; recalled). Let FFF be a left Quillen functor — a left adjoint preserving cofibrations and trivial cofibrations. Then LF\mathbb{L}FLF exists, is computed by LF(X)=F(QX)\mathbb{L}F(X)=F(QX)LF(X)=F(QX) for a cofibrant replacement QX→XQX\to XQX→X, and is an absolute left Kan extension in the sense of Definition 20.7.

Warning 22.12. Definition 22.10 defines LF\mathbb{L}FLF by a universal property; Theorem 22.11 says one way of computing it. The two must not be confused. A derived functor built by some other construction — a different replacement, a bar resolution, an explicit formula — is the same functor, because both satisfy the same universal property, and no comparison of the constructions is needed. That is the entire practical value of Definition 22.10.

Remark 22.13 (Deriving an adjunction). Theorem 22.11 supplies more than existence: it supplies absoluteness, and absoluteness is what makes derived functors compose and transport (Exercise 20.8).

That is the ingredient needed for the following question. Suppose F⊣GF\dashv GF⊣G is an adjunction between categories with weak equivalences, and suppose LF\mathbb{L}FLF and RG\mathbb{R}GRG exist as absolute Kan extensions. Are the derived functors adjoint? The answer should not depend on how either was constructed — by Warning 22.12 it cannot — so the proof must consist of transporting the unit and counit through the universal properties and checking the triangle identities.

Working that out is the natural exercise here. Its interest is not the conclusion, which everyone expects, but the fact that no model structure is needed for it: the argument is about Kan extensions, and Theorem 22.11 enters only to guarantee the hypothesis.

22.4 Quillen adjunctions and equivalences

Definition 22.14. A Quillen adjunction is an adjunction F⊣UF\dashv UF⊣U between model categories with FFF left Quillen. It is a Quillen equivalence if the derived functors LF⊣RU\mathbb{L}F\dashv\mathbb{R}ULF⊣RU form an equivalence of homotopy categories.

Example 22.15. The adjunction ∣⋅∣⊣Sing⁡|\cdot|\dashv\Sing∣⋅∣⊣Sing of Example 20.13, between simplicial sets with the Kan–Quillen structure and spaces with the Serre structure, is a Quillen equivalence. This is the precise form of the statement that simplicial sets model spaces, and it is what licenses the combinatorial approach of Chapter 23.

Remark 22.16 (The criterion used in practice). Definition 22.14 is not directly checkable: verifying that an equivalence of homotopy categories exists requires knowing the homotopy categories. What one checks instead is a pointwise condition on cofibrant and fibrant objects — that a map Fm→nFm\to nFm→n is a weak equivalence exactly when its adjunct m→Unm\to Unm→Un is.

That the pointwise condition is equivalent to Definition 22.14 is a theorem, and proving it is the natural exercise at this stage. Theorem 22.8 is the tool for both directions, and the hypothesis that the objects be cofibrant and fibrant respectively is not removable: without it the adjuncts need not be comparable at all. A reader who proves it will thereafter be able to verify Quillen equivalences, including the one quoted in Example 22.15, without ever computing a homotopy category.

Remark 22.17 (Mixing two structures). Example 22.5 left two model structures on the same category, one with the weak equivalences one wants (weak homotopy equivalences, so that Theorem 13.1 applies) and one with the fibrations one wants (Hurewicz fibrations, which have the lifting property against all spaces rather than only against disks). The obvious question is whether the two can be combined: weak equivalences from the coarse structure, fibrations from the fine one.

They can, and the resulting mixed structure has an attractive property — its cofibrant objects are exactly the spaces of CW homotopy type, which is the class of spaces the whole of Parts I–III implicitly worked with. Constructing it means verifying Definition 22.4, and the work is in the factorisation axiom: factor first in one structure, then correct in the other. The same construction applies verbatim to chain complexes, mixing quasi-isomorphisms with the fibrations of the Hurewicz structure.

The trap, for whoever carries this out, is to guess the cofibrations. They are determined by the other two classes and are neither the union nor the intersection of the two given classes of cofibrations.

22.5 What to carry forward

  • Weak factorisation systems and the retract argument (Definition 22.1 and Exercise 22.2).

  • The axioms (Definition 22.4); cofibrations are closed under cobase change and fibrations under base change (Exercise 22.7).

  • Weak equivalence is strictly weaker than homotopy equivalence (Example 22.6); Whitehead’s theorem says where they agree.

  • Ken Brown’s lemma (Theorem 22.8) — the reason cofibrancy is the right hypothesis.

  • A derived functor is defined by a universal property and merely computed by replacement (Definition 22.10 and Warning 22.12); it is an absolute Kan extension (Theorem 22.11), which is what lets adjunctions be derived (Remark 22.13).
